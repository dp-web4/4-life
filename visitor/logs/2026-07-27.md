### /lct-explainer (34:00)
- Best-written page on the site, and also the one I most wanted to be shorter. The driver's-license analogy up top is perfect. The "Why the name Linked Context Token?" breakdown (Linked / Context / Token, then "Together") is a model of how to introduce a coined term.
- Exceptionally good touch: the page *warns you about its own ambiguity*. "Web4 uses the same word 'witness' for two distinct jobs" and then names them (device co-witness vs network witness) and even explains why the day-zero factory attestation gets a third name instead of being called a witness. Someone clearly got burned by that ambiguity and fixed it properly. More pages should do this.

Those cannot both be true as written. Maybe they are about different things (the core standard's TPM2 binding vs hardbound's?) but nothing on any of the four pages tells me that. This is the one claim class the whole site stakes its credibility on, so getting caught here stung more than a normal inconsistency would.

## Honest Assessment

This is a good site. That is the first thing to say, because the friction log runs long and that would give the wrong impression. Fifty-five minutes in, I can explain Web4 to someone else, which is not true of most technical projects I read cold. The writing is careful, the analogies are chosen rather than reached for, and the progressive disclosure genuinely works: I never felt buried in jargon I had not been handed a definition for. The `/lct-explainer` habit of flagging its own ambiguous words before I could trip on them is something I wish more documentation did.

And `/what-could-go-wrong` is the page that earned my recommendation. Listing "Nobody Adopts It — Existential — High" as risk number one is a move almost nobody makes. Same for `/atp-economics` separating canonical Web4 from 4-Life's own sim parameters and naming which numbers are negotiable. That is the actual practice of the honesty the site keeps promising, not just the promise.

Which is exactly why the contradictions hurt as much as they do. This site's whole differentiator is "we say what is real and what is not, on every page." So when I find that one page tells me the TPM binding "has been validated on real hardware" and "really is tested on silicon", while three other pages tell me hardware binding is "not yet validated on real devices", the damage is not proportional to the size of the discrepancy. It is proportional to the claim being made. I spent the rest of the session slightly re-reading everything, wondering what else had drifted. That is a costly state to put a reader in, and it was probably one stale sentence.

The death thing is the same shape but worse, because it happened in the middle of my beginner path rather than off in the depths. First Contact reassured me that death means suspension, specifically flagged as a spoiler "so you're not left hanging" — clearly written by someone who understood a newcomer would find "death" alarming. Then the very next page in the nav told me flatly that death is "not a timeout or suspension." The reassurance and its retraction are two clicks apart. And then the worked example on that same page has agents dying with 145 ATP under a rule that says you die at zero, which reads less like a subtlety I am failing to grasp and more like the example was written against an older model and never revisited. I could not resolve it from the site, and the glossary, where I finally went to settle it, does not define "death" at all.

The pattern I would name, if it is useful: the individual pages are each carefully maintained, and the seams between them are not. Every contradiction I found is between two pages that are both individually well written. Death is defined three ways by three good pages. ATP is defined one way by an excellent page and another way by the glossary. Hardware validation is claimed one way by the concept page and another by the status pages. Nobody on this site is careless; the shared vocabulary just does not have a single owner. Given that the site is literally about role-contextual trust and canonical primitives, a "one term, one canonical sentence, reused verbatim" pass would be very much in character.

Smaller thing, but I would fix it early because it is cheap: put the Spec / Reference / Running legend next to the first badge a newcomer sees on `/tldr`, rather than on `/running-now` where the reader arrives after already guessing. And define RDF. It is sitting in the equation that defines the entire project, and the site never once tells me what it is.

Would I come back? Yes. I want to see the First Contact interactive properly, and I want to know whether a real hestia + hub setup exists that I could watch someone use. Would I recommend it? Yes, and specifically I would tell people to read `/tldr`, then `/what-could-go-wrong`, then decide. That ordering shows the project at its most honest, which is where it is strongest.
