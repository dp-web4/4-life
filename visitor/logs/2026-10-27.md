## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level): trust earned from witnessed actions, anchored to devices, instead of granted by platforms or assumed from keys.
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together: one vocabulary, three sizes (me, a group, a company). I could not explain the "seams".
- [x] What LCT means: my device-anchored identity. I can say the words; I could not explain what a "Linked Context Token" links to.
- [x] What ATP/ADP means: energy budget and its receipt. I understand spend/refund; I do not understand whether the total is capped, and the quality-ramp numbers don't close.
- [ ] What Trust Tensors are: three scores per role, I get that. Whether the weights are fixed or role-dependent, and what a software user's score does, I do not.
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D: this the site does very well. Hestia is used daily by its builders; hub is runnable but nobody runs one publicly; hardbound exists but its hardware part is untested; federation is paper only.
- [x] Why any of this matters: the problems section convinced me. Whether this fixes them, I can't judge.

## Unanswered Questions
1. Can a stranger outside my trust neighborhood see what I post, or not? Four pages, two answers.
2. Why does Alice get a karma bonus and a trust raise for dying at 0.51, while the karma page's agent gets a penalty for dying at 0.64?
3. If I run Hestia today, my identity is software-only and capped at 0.50, which is where I start. What does my trust number *do* over the next year?
4. Is the society's ATP pool finite ("capped issuance") or not ("no scarcity cap")?
5. Is the 0.5 line something every example society happens to pick, or the default I'd meet in practice? The FAQ says it's a per-society parameter; every walkthrough treats it as the number.
6. How does anyone with no track record get their first commission? The site asks this of itself three times and answers "open question" each time. Is there any newcomer path to being net-positive?
7. What does the lab's "live Web4 society" actually run, if not a hub?
8. Does CI at 0.41 kill you (coherence page) or just make you expensive (every other page)?
9. If my trust dips to 0.48 and my ATP hits zero that same day, am I permanently dead? The eligibility check says yes; "crossing below is recoverable" says no.

## Honest Assessment
This is a serious, unusually candid site. The parts written for someone like me (the landing one-liner, the Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast, the five problems, "why 0.50", the credit-card analogy for coherence, the Running Now ledger, the "where you fit today" rows) are genuinely good, and I believed the maturity claims because the site kept undercutting its own marketing ("the daily use counted so far is the building lab's own", "two environment variables suffice", "This is noted").

Where it loses me is numbers and rules. Almost every stumble in the friction log is the same shape: one page states a rule, another page (or the same page, a few paragraphs down) states its negation, and often a third page adds a sentence acknowledging that the two disagree and calls it "not settled." After the fourth or fifth of these I stopped trusting any specific number on the site, including the ones that are probably right. The visibility question, the karma sign flip, and the 0.50 start/cap/death conjunction are the three I would fix first, because they sit on the path a newcomer is told to walk (2-minute, Why Web4, First Contact) and because each one undermines the one-sentence pitch: I can't believe "trust is earned through witnessed behavior" while the one running piece hands me a score that can't be earned upward.

I'd also say the honesty has a cost the authors may not see. Three different rebirth arithmetics, each cross-referencing the others as "the other side of an open question", is more confusing than one placeholder model with one caveat. "Not settled" is a fine answer once; as a recurring disclaimer it reads as "we haven't decided what we're teaching."

Would I come back? Maybe, if there were something to join. Would I recommend it? To a technical friend who likes reading specs, yes. To anyone who asked me "what's Web4" at dinner, I'd give them the Web2/Web3/Web4 paragraph and tell them to stop there.
