## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level): a proposal that trust should come from observed, signed records of behaviour rather than from a platform or a key
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together: one shared vocabulary, three sizes to run it
- [x] What LCT means: a device-anchored identity key with a changeable label
- [ ] What ATP/ADP means: I understand the metaphor (energy spent, receipt returned, recharge on confirmation) but the prices contradict each other across four pages and I could not build a stable mental model of what anything costs
- [x] What Trust Tensors are: three numbers per role; talent never decays, the others do
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D: the ledger on /running-now is clear, with the hub-badge exception above
- [x] Why any of this matters: the problem pages convinced me; the solution pages half-convinced me

## Unanswered Questions
1. Does a stranger outside my three hops see my post or not? Three pages say visible-with-no-weight, one bullet says invisible.
2. If I only read, do I run out of energy and die without ever posting?
3. Why is spam priced below a quality post on every price list when the whole argument is that spam is expensive?
4. When I am reborn, does my trust go up (First Contact), down by 5% (karma formula), or carry over (How It Works)? And why is energy death worth +12 karma on one page and −15 on another?
5. The lab runs a hub every day. Why is the hub not "Running"?
6. What is "MCP" in the equation on /the-standard, and why is it the only term with no page of its own on the reading path?
7. Who are the people behind this? The site says "the lab" and links to a page of eight computers.
8. Is there anything a non-developer can *do* with this today, other than read? (/running-now says no, plainly. I would have liked that sentence on the landing page.)

## Honest Assessment
This is the most honest technical site I have read in a long time, and that honesty is both its strength and its main usability problem. Every page says what is real and what is not, and I believed it. But the site hedges so much about its own numbers ("one modelling choice, not the rule", "not settled", "read it for the shape, not the amount") that by /how-it-works I had stopped trusting any figure, which is a strange outcome for a site about trust. If a number is illustrative, it would help more to show *one* illustration consistently across pages than to show three and disclaim all of them.

The problem framing (Web2 vs Web3 vs Web4, the five "problems you already know") is excellent and I would send it to anyone. The mechanism explanation is good page by page and inconsistent page to page: visibility, spam price, post price, karma sign, risk count, system count all change depending on which page I am on. None of these is hard to understand on its own; the friction is in noticing that the page I just left said something else.

The thing I kept wanting and did not get: one plain list, early, of what an action costs and what it pays back, that every later page then obeys. The site has the pieces for that list; it just has four versions of it.

I would come back if I heard the numbers had been reconciled. I would recommend /tldr and /why-web4's first half to anyone today.
