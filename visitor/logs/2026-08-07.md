## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00 – 00:04)

- **First impression**: Clean, and unusually direct. "Trust, built into the internet." followed
  immediately by "A proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority,
  and by what rules." I knew within about eight seconds what the site claimed to be about, which
  is rare.
- The thing that made me trust the page: it defines "witness" inline, in the hero, before using
  it. "To witness is to observe an action and attest to it, staking your own reputation on the
  record." I did not have to go look that up.
- The four cards (core standard / hub / hestia / hardbound) read as four products, and I was not
  sure at first whether I was supposed to pick one or use all four. The intro sentence answers
  this ("The core standard is the substrate... three scales of the same posture") but I had to
  re-read it. Minor.
- The reference block at the bottom ("Web4 is research and development, not a finished product")
  did more to make me keep reading than the hero did. A landing page that leads with a caveat
  reads as honest rather than promotional.
- **Clicked**: "Web4 in 2 minutes" (/tldr), following the page's own suggested order.

### Web4 in 2 Minutes (00:04 – 00:11)

- The single most useful paragraph on the whole site is here: "**First, what is this site?**
  4-Life is an educational onramp to Web4: a set of explainers (this site, nothing you install)."
  I had genuinely been unsure whether "4-Life" was a product I was being sold. It is not, and the
  page said so before I had to ask.
- The Web2 / Web3 / Web4 three-way contrast landed cleanly. "A platform *declares* your
  reputation" vs "whoever holds the key *is assumed to be* the rightful owner" vs "trust accrues
  from *witnessed behavior*" is the clearest framing of the pitch I saw anywhere.
- Badges: the page shows **Reference** and **Running** and defines both, then says "See the full
  three-tier key (Spec, Reference, Running)". I noticed a third tier named but not defined here.
  That is fine — it links out — but for two paragraphs I wondered whether I had missed something.
- "Hestia — Running" with the immediate qualifier "The daily use counted so far is the building
  lab's own, so this badge does not claim adoption" is exactly the kind of sentence that made me
  stop looking for the catch. Good.
- **Clicked**: "Why Web4?" per the reading path.

### Why Web4? (00:11 – 00:29)

This is where the reading path's time estimate stopped matching reality for me.

- The first two thirds (five problems, four failed prior solutions, five required properties, four
  proposed mechanisms) is well-paced and I read it straight through in about seven minutes. The
  problem-before-solution ordering works. "Root cause: Actions have no cost" as a repeated closer
  on each problem is a good device.
- Then the Honest Questions section opens with an index of **68 questions across seven topics**.
  The page does warn me ("Reference depth, not read-through"), and I appreciated that, but the
  reading path told me this was step 2 of a five-page, ~30-minute journey. I ended up spending
  18 minutes here alone and I still only read maybe a third of the Q&A. I did not feel misled,
  but I did feel like the path's time budget and this page's actual mass are describing different
  documents.
- Quality of the Q&A itself is high. "Is this deployed anywhere?" answers with a real ledger and
  a "What's NOT built yet" list. "Isn't this just a social credit system?" has a comparison table
  *and* an honest caveat that "the protocol doesn't prevent oppressive configuration." I did not
  catch the site spinning anything.
- **First real snag.** Under "What Would Actually Work", required property #2 reads: "**Make
  identity expensive to fake** - Physical hardware required." Flat, no hedge. But further down the
  same page, the privacy section offers "**Software-only LCT** (medium)" as a recommended
  pseudonymity option, and the identity section says plainly "software-only identities are cheap."
  So on one page I was told hardware is *required* and then told how to participate without it.
  I could not tell which was the real rule until three pages later.
- **Second snag, which I chased for the rest of the session.** The FAQ "Take month off — trust
  gone?" reassures: Talent doesn't decay, Training 180-day half-life, Temperament 30-day half-life,
  and "Recovers quickly once active." Then it volunteers the six-month case: "Temperament
  essentially zeroed (~1.5% remains). Training ~50%. Talent unchanged." I filed that away without
  alarm. It became alarming two pages later.
- **Clicked**: "First Contact."

- Act 5 is titled "Die & Reborn" with "ATP=0, +12 bonus", while the trust-score note says of the

- Could not drive the walkthrough (see caveat above).

### How It Works (00:35 – 00:44)

- This page resolved most of my accumulated confusion, and it is the best-written page on the site
  for someone who has read the first three.
- "**Two ways to die, and they are very different.** Two paths: ATP hits zero (energy death) or
  raw trust falls below 0.5 and stays there (trust death)." Followed by "One line, two
  consequences: *crossing* below 0.5 restricts your features right away and is recoverable; only
  *staying* below it is fatal - a sustained collapse, not a single stumble." That is the sentence
  I needed on First Contact.
- The rebirth eligibility explanation is genuinely good teaching. It shows the arithmetic
  (`0.4(0.40) + 0.3(0.60) + 0.3(0.60) = 0.52`) and explicitly heads off the wrong reading ("*not*
  each dimension clearing 0.5 on its own").
- The "**About these numbers**" disclosure on the three-lives example is the most impressive
  paragraph I read all session. The page notices that its own worked example implies a cause of
  death the standard does not define, says so, and refuses to invent one: "rather than invent a
  third cause to justify the figures below, this page declines to name one." I have never seen an
  explainer flag its own illustration as unlicensed. It cost me nothing in comprehension and
  bought a lot of credibility.
- **Where the alarm went off.** This page gives me the survival rule. /why-web4 gave me the decay
  rates and a worked six-month absence. Nobody on the site puts those two facts in the same room.
  So I did it myself, using only the site's own numbers, for the site's own best-case user:

  > A user with Talent 0.90, Training 0.90, Temperament 0.90 goes inactive for six months.
  > Talent does not decay: **0.90**. Training at a 180-day half-life: **0.45**. Temperament at a
  > 30-day half-life, six half-lives elapsed: **0.014**.
  > Composite, at the canonical weights this page states: `0.4(0.90) + 0.3(0.45) + 0.3(0.014)` = **0.499**.

  Below the line. And "staying there" is satisfied by construction, because I am absent — that is
  what absence *is*. So on the site's own arithmetic, a near-perfectly-trusted person who takes
  parental leave, gets hospitalized, or does a six-month field posting appears to meet the
  definition of permanent, unrecoverable trust death. I do not think that is the intent. But I
  read four pages and could not find the sentence that rules it out.
- **Clicked**: "Running Now."

### Running Now (00:44 – 00:50)

- Best "what's real" page I have encountered on a project site. The badge key is unambiguous, and
  the extra paragraph distinguishing **pilot-ready** ("a statement made *inside* Reference... It
  does not claim any exist yet") is precisely the distinction that most projects blur.
- "**Whose machines those are**": "the deployment counted here is the lab's. Daily use by the
  people who build it is what the Running badge claims, and it does not claim adoption." That is
  a project marking down its own headline claim, voluntarily.
- "**What the chain records**" volunteers that Hestia's witness chain misses any action that
  routes around the policy gate, that two environment variables suffice to do so, and that this is
  "an accepted limit right now rather than a scheduled repair." Disclosing your own accountability
  gap on the page that advertises your accountability layer is not a normal choice.
- The "Where you fit today" personas are the most useful navigation on the site. Persona 1 ("You
  are here to understand it, not to run it. Then nothing here needs installing for today to be
  worth your time") correctly identified me and told me I was done, which no other page did.

## Unanswered Questions

1. **If I go inactive for six months, do I die?** On the site's own decay rates and weights, even a
   0.90-across-the-board user lands at composite 0.499, and absence is a sustained condition. Is
   there a dormancy exemption, a decay floor, or a rule that only *acting* entities are judged
   against the survival line? This is the one question where I could not tell whether the answer is
   "no, obviously" or "yes, and nobody has noticed."
2. **What is a karma tier?** `/first-contact` says effective trust (raw × CI²) sets it. No page I
   read says what tiers exist or what they get you.
3. **Can a hub society include hardbound-tier members?** `/onramp` gives me hestia→hub and
   hestia→hardbound. The community-to-enterprise relationship is unaddressed.
4. **What does the 0.50-ceiling user actually experience day to day?** `/lct-explainer` answers the
   survival question well ("zero margin"), but if any dip restricts features until I climb back and
   I can never climb *above* the line, how often is a normal software-only participant restricted
   in practice? Is that tier usable, or is it nominal?
5. **Is trust death society-scoped or global?** The plagiarism walkthrough on `/how-it-works` says
   "the penalty is society-specific, not global." Trust death is described as permanent and
   reputation-wide. If I suffer trust death in one society, am I dead everywhere, given that
   `/why-web4` says communities are federated and forks preserve your history?
6. **What does "the community no longer trusts you" mean operationally at the moment of trust
   death?** `/lct-explainer` says posts, votes, and ATP transfers stop being accepted. Does the LCT
   persist as a tombstone, or is there nothing left? The energy-death case is explicit ("same LCT,
   same history"); the trust-death case is not.

## Honest Assessment

This site is doing something I have not seen before, and it is worth naming before the criticism:
**it argues against itself on purpose, in the places where that costs the most.** The Running badge
comes with a paragraph explaining that it does not mean adoption. The accountability-layer page
discloses that two environment variables defeat its own witness gate, and calls that "an accepted
limit today, not a scheduled repair." The lifecycle page notices that its own three-lives
illustration implies a death rule the standard does not define, and rather than inventing a
justification, writes "this page declines to name one." That last one is remarkable. Most
explainers would have quietly picked a plausible reason.

The effect on me as a reader was not skepticism, it was the opposite. By the third such disclosure
I had stopped hunting for the catch, which meant I read the substance instead of reading defensively.
If there is a single transferable lesson in this site's construction, that is it.

The criticism is narrower than the praise, and it has one shape: **the site is excellent at
disclosing each fact and inconsistent at joining two of them.** Every individual claim I chased
turned out to be stated somewhere, honestly, with its caveat attached. Twice I built up what I
thought was a serious finding and discovered the site had already shipped the answer — the
software-only zero-margin problem is answered head-on at `/lct-explainer#software-only-survival`,
and `/what-could-go-wrong` risk 3 pre-empts the ceiling-versus-survival-line confusion in a
parenthetical. I withdrew both. What survived is the case where two of the site's own facts, both
correct, both well-explained, sit on the same page and entail something neither of them says: the
decay rates and the survival line. The arithmetic is four terms long and every number is supplied
by the site. A reader who does it gets a frightening result about taking parental leave, and there
is nowhere on the site to go and check.

I would also note that `/what-could-go-wrong` risk 3 contains the exact reasoning that resolves
this, applied to a different case: "a scoring error is a sustained condition by construction, so a
wrongly-scored user is precisely the one who stays below." Somebody on this project has already
had the thought that "staying below" catches people who are not doing anything wrong. It just has
not been pointed at absence yet.

Structurally, the reading path works and I would tell someone else to follow it. `/tldr` →
`/why-web4` → `/how-it-works` → `/running-now` was the load-bearing sequence for me; First Contact
sat between two of those and, in static form, mostly prepared me for something I could not then
do. The one navigational thing I would change is `/karma-consequences`: two pages route readers
there for the lifecycle and the appeals process, and it delivers neither.

Net: I arrived knowing nothing and left able to explain Web4 to someone else, including what is
not true about it yet. That is a high bar and the site clears it.
