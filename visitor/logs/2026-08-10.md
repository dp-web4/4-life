## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- **First impression**: Clean, and the headline does actual work. "Trust, built into the internet." followed by "A proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority, and by what rules." I knew what the site was about in under ten seconds, which is rare.
- The word **"proposed"** in the subheading is doing a lot of honest work and I noticed it immediately. Good.
- **Clicked**: "Web4 in 2 minutes."
- **Confusion points**: The four onramp cards (core standard / hub / hestia / hardbound) meant nothing to me yet, but they were clearly labelled "Substrate / Community scale / Personal scale / Enterprise scale," so I could park them without anxiety. That's the right call — I didn't feel like I was being quizzed.

### Web4 in 2 Minutes (02:10)
- Genuinely the best page on the site. "Plain English first, so you can read this cold" is a promise it actually keeps.
- The **Web2 / Web3 / Web4 three-way contrast** is what made the idea click for me. "A platform *declares* your reputation" / "whoever holds the key *is assumed to be* the rightful owner" / "trust accrues from *witnessed behavior*." I understood the pitch from that alone.
- I appreciated being told, unprompted, that **4-Life is not one of the four pieces** — "which 4-Life teaches but is not one of." I had actually started to wonder whether the site was itself a product. It answered before I asked.
- The **"How real is it, honestly?"** section is disarming. `pip install web4-core` in the middle of a research pitch is a strong move — it made me believe the rest of the page more.
- **No confusion here.** I want to be explicit about that, because the rest of this log is friction and I don't want the ratio to misrepresent the experience.

- Then I hit the FAQ and it is **enormous** — I lost count somewhere past sixty questions across six or seven sections. I skimmed rather than read, which I suspect is not the intent.

## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level) — witnessed behavior as the basis of trust, versus platform-declared (Web2) or key-assumed (Web3). The three-way contrast on `/tldr` did this.
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together — one substrate, three scales (personal / community / enterprise). Clear by `/onramp`.
- [x] What LCT means — Linked Context Token; device-anchored identity, hardware fingerprint plus changeable label, pseudonymous.
- [x] What ATP/ADP means — Allocation Transfer Packets (spendable energy budget) and Allocation Discharge Packets (the receipt). The cell-biology analogy carried it.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are — T3, three role-scoped dimensions: Talent, Training, Temperament. **Understood conceptually, but I do not trust the page's worked numbers** (see friction log).
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D — `/running-now` is unusually clear on this, including what the badges *don't* claim.
- [x] Why any of this matters — AI agents already act; there's no verifiable record of what they did, on whose authority, under which rules.

Seven of seven, which I did not expect going in. The two things I'd flag: my T3 understanding is conceptual only because the arithmetic didn't check out, and the rebirth rules I understand *despite* `/how-it-works`, not because of it.

## Unanswered Questions

1. **Does hitting ATP=0 disqualify me from rebirth or not?** The `/how-it-works` diagram says any one failure means "no rebirth"; the same page and all of First Contact say energy death is recoverable. These cannot both be true and I don't know which to believe.
2. **What is the actual rebirth threshold, and is it inclusive?** `/how-it-works` says "Overall T3 score ≥ 0.5." A software-only identity is capped at exactly 0.50. So such a user sits permanently *on* the threshold — passing by exactly zero margin, forever, and only while behaving perfectly. `/lct-explainer` and `/what-could-go-wrong` both own the ceiling honestly, but neither runs it forward into the rebirth rule. Is a software-only identity permanently one bad interaction from being ineligible to come back?
3. **Do appeals exist today, or are they a design?** And if designed-only, is it fair for the social-credit comparison table to list appeals as a live differentiator?
4. **Do roles actually weight the T3 dimensions differently?** The page says yes, the widget shows one set of weights, the worked examples imply a different, uniform set. I can't tell which reflects the real system.
5. **Which community-size numbers are right — 5-10 or 3-5?**
6. **Who witnesses the very first action?** I was told my own device is my "day-zero witness," and separately that "software-only identities can be witnessed but generally don't witness others." If I'm software-only, what is my day-zero witness?
7. **What does the 5% transfer burn fund, concretely?** I read that it "routes to the community redistribution pool" — who decides where that pool goes?
8. **What does any of this look like on a screen?** The FAQ admits "no production Web4 apps exist yet" and "the exact UX hasn't been built yet." Honest, but it's the thing I most wanted to see.

## Honest Assessment

This site is much better than I expected, and its main problem is not the one I usually find.

The usual failure mode for a project like this is hype — vague grandeur, no admission of limits. 4-Life has the opposite posture, aggressively so. `/running-now` tells me the only "Running" badge counts the builders' own four machines. `/what-could-go-wrong` tells me the design "codifies the existing digital divide into the trust layer itself." The hestia section tells me two environment variables bypass the accountability chain. I have never seen a project volunteer that much against itself, and it made me believe everything else on the site more, not less. If I recommend this to someone, that honesty is the reason.

The reading path works. `/tldr` is excellent and I understood the core pitch from one page. By the end I could answer all seven checklist items, which surprised me.

So the caveat on "would recommend" is narrow but real: **the two places I stopped believing the site were both places where the site contradicted itself, not places where it was hard.** That's a different and more damaging kind of friction, because the whole value proposition here is "we tell you the truth about what's real." When the aliveness diagram told me energy death means no rebirth, forty seconds after First Contact walked me through Alice's energy death *and rebirth*, I didn't think "this is complex" — I thought "someone isn't checking." Same with the trust tensor: I tried to reproduce one number, couldn't, worked out that all three examples use a flat weighting the page never mentions, and briefly wondered whether the role-specific thesis was real at all. It probably is. But a page that teaches "trust comes from verifiable records" should survive having its own arithmetic verified.

Both of these are small fixes. Neither is a design problem. That's what makes them worth fixing first — a site that has already done the hard part (being honest about a research project) is losing credibility on two errors that a careful read catches in under an hour.

One last observation. The single most useful sentence I read all session was on `/lct-explainer`: "With software-only hardware, 0.5 is both where you start and the highest you can reach." I had worked my way to that worry independently and was braced to be told I'd misunderstood. Instead the site had already written it down, and then called the tradeoff "a policy choice, not a technical inevitability." That's the site at its best — it had anticipated the reader who takes it seriously enough to be alarmed. I'd like the rebirth rules and the trust tensor examples to be held to that same standard.
