## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level): a proposed open standard where trust comes from witnessed, recorded behavior instead of a platform's say-so or possession of a key. Taught by the site (/tldr, /why-web4).
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together: one standard, three sizes (personal, community, enterprise). Taught by the site. I am less sure what the hub actually does all day.
- [x] What LCT means: Linked Context Token, an identity tied to a device (ideally its security chip) and vouched for by other devices. Taught. The exact format and the two ceiling rules are fuzzy.
- [~] What ATP/ADP means: ATP is an energy budget you spend to act and get refunded when others confirm your work; ADP is the receipt. The concept was taught. The numbers do not hold together, so I could not predict what would happen to my balance.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are: three scores (Talent, Training, Temperament) per role, which fade at different rates. Taught clearly; the best concept page.
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D: installable packages, a hub you can run, Hestia in daily use by its own authors on 4 machines, Hardbound not validated on real hardware, no public network, federation unbuilt. Taught very well (/running-now).
- [~] Why any of this matters: yes for AI agent accountability and spam. I am not convinced it matters to me personally soon, since there is no network to join and no app to see.

## Unanswered Questions
1. If I die of energy exhaustion with good trust, do I come back better off (+12), the same (whole balance), or worse off (−15 karma, 70 ATP)?
2. Can a stranger outside my neighborhood see my posts or not?
3. Where does ATP come from in the first place, and at what rate does a community issue it? Is issuance capped or not?
4. How does a newcomer whose own posts can at best break even ever get ahead, if the first commission is an open question?
5. What does a software-only identity (the only kind I can get today from Hestia) actually experience, living at 0.50 with no room above?
6. Does the "death and rebirth" story apply to humans, or only to AI agents?
7. Does the lab's own society run on the hub software? If yes, why is the hub not badged Running?
8. Who is behind this? The FAQ says the anonymity is deliberate and admits the tension. For a project about trust and accountability that is a strange place to be anonymous.
9. If the hub cannot check that a member's hardware is real hardware, what do the hardware trust tiers mean inside a hub today?
10. What is a "society" in practice: a Discord server, a company, a group chat? What would the first one outside the lab look like?
11. What would any of this look like on a screen for an ordinary person? (I saw references to wireframes on /day-in-web4 but did not get there.)
12. Why is the cheapest thing to publish a spam message?

## Honest Assessment
The site is good at two things that most projects like this are bad at: telling me why I should care before telling me how it works, and telling me what is not real yet. /tldr, the first half of /why-web4, /trust-tensor and /running-now are clear enough that I could explain them to someone else tonight. The witness definition on the landing page, the Web2 / Web3 / Web4 contrast, the résumé-and-reference-check analogy, and "stops accidents, not adversaries" are the things I will remember.

Where it lost me was numbers. Almost every page has worked examples, and I checked them because the site invites it. The trust arithmetic holds up everywhere I looked. The energy and karma arithmetic does not: the quality ramp disagrees with its own table, the two-contributor example ends with both of them broke, and the same death produces a bonus on one page, a penalty on another and a full carry-over on a third. Each page carefully says "this is one modelling choice, not settled", and I believe that is true, but the cumulative effect of five separate disclaimers is that I stopped believing any number. I would rather be shown one consistent toy model, labelled once as a toy, than three honest and incompatible ones.

The second thing that nagged was a gap between the story and what I can touch. Every narrative follows a hardware-anchored identity climbing to 0.6, 0.7, 0.9. The only thing running today gives me a software identity that starts, peaks and dies at the same 0.50. The site says this openly in several places, which I respect, but it never shows me that life. The reader the site most wants (someone who installs Hestia this week) is the one reader whose experience is never illustrated.

The third is length and repetition. I read the "two ways to die" explanation, nearly word for word, on at least five pages, and the "Running: hestia and the hub are public AGPL code, no live network yet" callout on almost all of them. By /how-it-works I was skimming. The 72-question FAQ is impressive as a reference and exhausting as a waypoint.

Would I come back? If I were building AI agents, yes, for /running-now and /hestia specifically. As a curious general reader, I got the idea in the first fifteen minutes and the following hour mostly added doubt about the details. That may be the honest state of the project. But the fifteen-minute version is the one I would recommend, and I would tell people to stop there.
