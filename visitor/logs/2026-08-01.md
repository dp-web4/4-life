## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- **First impression**: Clean and unusually direct. "Trust, built into the internet." Then immediately the concrete claim: AI agents write code, move money, make decisions, and "we cannot prove what an agent did, on whose authority, or by what rules." That sentence did more work than any headline I've read on a project site. I knew what problem I was looking at inside fifteen seconds.
- The definition of "witness" is given inline on the landing page: "To witness is to observe an action and attest to it, staking your own reputation on the record." Good. I didn't have to go hunting.
- **Clicked**: "Web4 in 2 minutes" (/tldr). The two CTAs are well chosen: one for "explain it" and one for "show me the parts."
- **Confusion points**: none, really. The landing page is short, which I appreciated. The nav is large (four sections, ~25 links) and I ignored it entirely at this stage.

### /tldr — Web4 in 2 Minutes (02:10)

### /what-could-go-wrong (56:30)
- **This is the page that made me take the project seriously.** Nine risks with severity and likelihood, each with mitigations and then a separately-labelled "honest assessment" that usually walks the mitigation back. Risk 6 concludes "witness governance is one of the hardest unsolved problems." - Risk 9 is remarkable: the one piece badged Running has a disclosed, reproducible policy-gate bypass (two environment variables, published as issue #49), and two of the bypasses are stated to be undetectable by any mechanism the team has or plans, because the gate runs inside the process it governs — "governance and not containment." A project volunteering that about its own flagship is not something I expected. I checked, and /hestia does carry the same admission on its own page rather than hiding it here.
- **But this page also contains the discrepancy that damaged my confidence most.** Risk 4's whole argument is that fake identities are expensive: "$500 in hardware" each, "$500K for 1,000 identities." /why-web4's affordability FAQ argues the opposite direction with the same quantity: a $50 phone, a $25 YubiKey. If the FAQ is right, 1,000 identities costs $25K-$50K, not $500K, and Risk 4's central number is off by 10-20x. Both arguments are load-bearing and they need the hardware price to be different things.

Read plainly, 0.93 means getting back 93 cents on the dollar — so honest participation also loses money, just 3% less badly than cheating. The page itself concedes "the margin is small." I don't know what these numbers mean and I'm not sure they say what the section wants them to say.

### /value-tensor (66:00)

- The anti-lying argument is structural: each of the three dimensions is scored by a different mechanism (recipient / witness attestation / automated structural checks), so "no single party controls all three dimensions." That answers a lone liar. It does not answer a recipient and a witness who are the same person's two accounts, or who simply agree to trade favors. The page has no collusion section.

### /trust-neighborhood (67:00)
- MRH = Markov Relevancy Horizon, with the disarming admission that it's "a math-flavored term we keep for spec-level work; you don't need it to use the idea." More sites should do that.
- Numbers: direct 0.70, two hops 0.49, three hops 0.34, beyond three hops 0 and invisible. Formula `trust = t₁ × t₂ × t₃ × 0.7^depth`. Each hop "costs 30% of the remaining trust."
- **This is where the hop contradiction bit me.** /how-it-works had taught me 90% retention per hop over a 5-hop chain. Here it's 70% per hop with a hard cutoff at 3. Same word, two rates, two limits. I went back and forth between the pages twice before concluding they must mean different things by "hop" — but neither page says so, and I had to infer it.
- Also a smaller thing: why is a *direct* connection worth only 0.70? Someone I know personally gets a 30% haircut before their own trust score is even applied. That's the first hop of a decay curve, but it reads as "the site doesn't fully trust anyone."

### /glossary and /coherence-index (68:00)
- The "Acronyms at a glance" index is useful and covers ATP, ADP, LCT, T3, V3, CI, MRH, MCP, RDF with one-line expansions. **Missing: R6, R7, SAL, RBAC** — and R6/R7 are exactly the ones I met unexpanded on /onramp and went to the index to look up.
- Coherence Index: four dimensions (spatial, capability, temporal, relational), geometric mean so one weak axis drags everything, new accounts start at 0.85 rather than 1.0 "because there's no established pattern yet." Nice detail.
- **A wrinkle I couldn't resolve**: the page reassures me that legitimate life changes are safe because "real life changes affect one or two dimensions at a time. An attacker taking over your account affects all four at once." But the worked example demonstrating the mechanism is `CI = (0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.1)^(1/4) = 0.52` — that's *one* bad dimension, i.e. the pattern the text just told me is the innocent one, producing a heavily punished score. The example and the reassurance argue against each other.

## Unanswered Questions
1. **How much does a hardware-backed identity actually cost?** $25, $50, or $500? The site needs it cheap to answer the equity objection and expensive to answer the Sybil objection, and quotes both without reconciling them.
2. **Where is hardbound?** It is described in detail, credited with 300+ integration tests and a specific binary size, called open source, and linked only to an org page that doesn't contain it.
3. **What is daily life like at a 0.50 ceiling?** I can post, earn ATP and build karma, but I sit exactly on the line whose crossing restricts me, permanently, with no way to accumulate margin. Does one bad interaction restrict me? How do I get back if every point of recovery is capped at the line I'm already on?
4. **Is a "hop" one thing or two?** 90% per hop over five hops, or 70% per hop with a hard wall at three?
5. **What does ROI mean in risk 4, and why is the honest strategy also below 1.0?**
6. **What stops two parties from scoring each other favorably?** V3's separation-of-mechanism argument stops one liar, not two cooperating.
7. **What do R6 and R7 stand for?** Glossed but never expanded, and absent from the acronym index.
8. **If nothing has a live network, who am I being witnessed by?** Hestia runs locally, the hub is pilot-ready with no users, hardbound is unreachable. Witnessing needs other participants, and I couldn't work out where they'd come from on day one of a real deployment.

## Honest Assessment

This is the most intellectually honest project site I've read, and my complaints are almost entirely about *consistency* rather than *candour*.

The candour is genuinely unusual. /what-could-go-wrong lists nine risks with severity and likelihood, and each one ends with an "honest assessment" paragraph that walks its own mitigations back. Risk 9 discloses that the single piece badged Running has a policy-gate bypass the team can't detect and doesn't plan to be able to detect, with an issue number. Risk 8 states outright that the accessibility problem is unsolved and that the design's position on it is "contestable." /how-it-works volunteers that its appeals process "hasn't been tested with real humans yet." Nothing forced them to write any of that. I finished the site trusting the authors more than I trust most shipped products, which is a strange thing to say about something with no users.

What undermines it is that the site argues with numbers, and the numbers don't hold still. The hardware price is the clearest case: the equity FAQ needs a hardware identity to cost $25-$50 so that the system isn't exclusionary, and the Sybil-resistance risk needs it to cost $500 so that fake identities are expensive. Both are asserted, neither acknowledges the other, and the whole security argument in risk 4 is off by an order of magnitude depending on which page you believe. The same thing happens with hops: 90% per hop on one page, 70% on another, five hops here, a hard limit of three there. And with 0.50, which is variously the survival line, the starting value, the software-only ceiling, the rebirth threshold, and the access boundary — described with a different comparison operator nearly every time it appears. Individually these are small. Collectively they taught me to stop trusting any specific figure and to read only the prose, which is a shame, because the prose is excellent and the figures are the site's evidence.

The other thing I'd flag is where the bad news lives. The site is not hiding anything, but it isn't always putting the disclosure where the decision is made. /lct-explainer told me that with one device and no security chip I'm "still in" — cheerful, welcoming, true as far as it goes. It did not tell me that if I lose that device there is nothing to recover to and I start from zero. That sentence exists, well written and unflinching, in risk 8 of a page called "What Could Go Wrong." A reader deciding whether the software-only tier is good enough for them will read the first page and may never reach the second. The honest material has already been written; some of it is just filed under "risks" when it belongs under "here's your option."

Two smaller notes. The "Running" badge is defined as "in day-to-day use" and evidenced by four lab machines — I'd rather the definition were narrowed than the fact inflated, and to the site's credit the fact wasn't inflated. And hardbound is the one place where I actively tried to check a claim and couldn't: a specific test count, a specific binary size, an open-source framing, and no repository at the end of the link. That's on the page aimed at organizations that need audit trails, who are exactly the people who will click.

Would I return? Yes. This taught me a genuinely new frame — that trust could be a property of *witnessed behavior* rather than of platforms or keys — in about ten minutes, and the RDF-as-backbone framing on /the-standard reorganized how I think about the whole thing. Would I recommend it? Maybe. I'd recommend /tldr and /what-could-go-wrong to anyone without hesitation.
