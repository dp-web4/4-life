## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- First impression: clean, and the one-liner is genuinely good — "a proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority, and by what rules." I knew immediately what problem this claims to solve, which is rare.
- The four-piece layout (standard / hub / hestia / hardbound) with plain-English taglines ("Named for the Greek goddess of the hearth," "As in hardware-bound") worked for me. The "Where this actually stands" honesty box near the bottom is unusual for a project site and made me more curious, not less.
- Clicked: "Web4 in 2 minutes," as the page told me to.
- Confusion points: none serious. The nav is enormous (24 links in the sidebar) but the page tells you to ignore it and read five pages in order, which I did.

### /tldr (04:00)
- The Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast is the best teaching device on the site. "A platform declares reputation" vs "key possession is assumed ownership" vs "trust accrues from witnessed behavior" — I got the shift in one read.
- The Spec / Reference / Running badge system is introduced here and it paid off all session.
- Confusion point: the badge explainer told me cards show "only the furthest achievement reached," and then in the same breath that the core standard card "carries both Spec and Reference badges." Those can't both be the rule as stated. /running-now later says plainly that a piece can carry more than one badge, which reads as the real rule — so the /tldr sentence about "only the furthest" is the odd one out. I'd double-check its exact wording; I may have compressed it in my notes, but I definitely stumbled there while reading.
- "How real is it, honestly?" — this section is why I kept reading. No timeline promises, stated as such.

### /why-web4 (12:00)
- The problem framing (spam is free, reputation is trapped, fresh starts are unlimited) is concrete and familiar.
- The FAQ is enormous. As a first-timer I read three entries: the social-credit comparison table (direct, best answer of its kind I've seen), the lost-hardware answer (honest about software-only users starting over from zero), and "what backs ATP" (the phone-minutes analogy landed).
- I did not attempt the other ~70 entries. The categorized index made that feel okay rather than overwhelming.

### /first-contact (24:00)
- The "two numbers to watch" framing (ATP energy, trust score) is exactly the right amount of setup.
- The story map spoils the ending on purpose and labels it as a choice ("skip it and press Start if you would rather find out by driving") — nice.
- I noticed and appreciated the fence-posting: Alice dies at ATP 0 with trust 0.51, which the intro's own rule (energy death is recoverable if you built trust) predicts — the numbers agree with the rules I'd just been taught.
- Small unanswered beat: Act 5's subtitle is "Overextending costs trust and energy," but the map never says what Alice *did* to overextend. I assume the walkthrough itself shows the choice; from the map alone the fatal act is a mystery.
- The karma caveat ("12 is one modelling choice, not the rule") appears twice. Slightly repetitive, but I'd rather that than false precision.

### /how-it-works (34:00)
- This page is the whole model, and it is long. It says so itself, which helps, but I still hit fatigue around the governance section.
- Things that worked: the five-system loop diagram, the "recharge refunds, payment earns" distinction (repeated until it stuck), the worked composite arithmetic (0.4×0.40 + 0.3×0.60 + 0.3×0.60 = 0.52 — I checked it, it's right), and the two-deaths distinction.
- Friction 1: the Key Takeaways box opens with "Every action costs energy, from about 1 ATP to read something up to 10-20 to post" — then two scrolls later the action table prices creating tasks at 15-30 ATP and broadcasting at 20-50 ATP. The takeaway reads like the full cost range and gets contradicted by the page's own table. "Posting" specifically is 10-20, so it's defensible, but as a summary sentence it set my expectation of the ceiling wrong.
- Friction 2: the trust-update section drills in that steps are "deliberately tiny" — the worked rule is 0.02 × (quality − 0.5), so at most about ±0.01 per action. Then the plagiarism walkthrough has the law oracle drop Dr. Chen's Training score by 0.15 in a single verdict — roughly 20× the biggest per-action step I'd just been taught. Nothing on the page says governance penalties are a different channel from the per-action update rule. I sat there wondering whether the oracle was breaking the rule the page had just made me memorize.
- The "Two different numbers, not two rates for one thing" box (chain trust 0.9-per-agent vs neighborhood 0.7-per-hop) answered a confusion I had actually formed two sections earlier. Well placed.

### /atp-economics (63:00)
- Thorough and mostly satisfying. "Recharge refunds, payment earns" is stated as a slogan and then actually demonstrated in the simulator numbers (commissioned tasks are the only net-positive actions). Consistent with everything upstream.
- The "What's Settled vs What's Still Moving" section is a great pattern — it told me which numbers to hold loosely (the 5%, the 7×) and which shapes to trust.

- Also: at 50% quality the table pays ~12 ATP on a 50-ATP task, but a linear ramp from 0 (at 30%) to ~30 (at 70%) would predict 15 at the midpoint. Small thing; the "~" absorbs it, but a numerate reader will do exactly this arithmetic.

### /what-could-go-wrong (68:00)
- The page that converted me from "interesting" to "I'd tell a friend about this." Nine risks with severity and likelihood, several graded "honest no." Risk 9 (the agent routes around its own safety gate) is *measured on their own running system* and published. Projects do not usually do this.
- It's also where the sharpest question of my session formed — see Unanswered Questions 1 and 2.

### /lct-explainer + /glossary (72:00)
- I came here chasing two threads: the software-only survival question, and R6/R7.
- The software-only section largely delivered: "0.5 is both where you start and the highest you can reach... any dip restricts your features until you climb back, and your reputation can never carry more weight than the line itself." Clear about the mechanics, carefully silent on the edge case (see UQ1).
- The hardware-tier table is crisp: TPM 0.90, Secure Enclave 0.85, FIDO2 0.75, software-only 0.50, with "these are ceilings, not starting points."
- **Then the glossary contradicted it.** The glossary's presence/LCT material says: "More devices co-signing = higher trust ceiling (1 device caps at 0.50, 2 devices at 0.75, 3+ up to 0.90)." So which is it? By the explainer's table, my single TPM-equipped phone has a 0.90 ceiling. By the glossary's sentence, *any* single device caps at 0.50 — the same ceiling as software-only. These are not two phrasings of one rule; they assign different numbers to the same setup, and no page I read reconciles them (a count axis multiplied with a chip axis? counts only within a chip tier? the table assuming 3+ devices?). This was my biggest "wait, what?" of the session, and it's in the two places a confused visitor is most likely to consult for the authoritative answer.
- R6/R7 definitions in the glossary are good (R6 routine grammar, R7 adds reputation back-propagation for consequential acts) — /onramp just never pointed me at them.

## Unanswered Questions
1. **Life exactly at the line, software-only.** Software-only trust starts at 0.50, is capped at 0.50, and staying *below* 0.50 is permanent death. The explainer says a dip "restricts your features until you climb back" — but a software-only user lives their entire life with zero buffer above the fatal line. Is one sustained rough patch enough to start the permanent-death clock for an honest software-only user? The site describes the mechanics of dipping and climbing but never says what long-run survival at the line actually looks like.
2. **Which axis sets my ceiling** — chip type or device count? (See HIGH friction row. I genuinely do not know which number applies to my own phone after reading both pages that should tell me.)
3. **What did Alice actually do in Act 5?** The story map says "overextending" kills her at ATP 0 / trust 0.51, but never names the choice. Presumably the driven walkthrough shows it; the map alone doesn't.
4. **Who decides an act is "consequential" (R7) vs routine (R6)?** The glossary distinguishes them cleanly but not who or what routes an action to one framework or the other.
5. **First commission for a newcomer** — the site flags this as open itself, repeatedly and honestly, so this is less "unanswered" than "answered with 'nobody knows yet,'" which I accept.

## Honest Assessment
This is the most honest project site I can remember reading. The pattern that builds trust is consistent: every claim of what exists is immediately qualified by what doesn't ("runnable is not the same as running in production," "the deployment counted here is the lab's," "two environment variables suffice" to bypass the running system's own safety gate — published by the team that built it). By the time I reached /what-could-go-wrong, the site had earned enough credibility that its self-criticism read as information rather than theater.

The teaching mostly works. The five-page path is well ordered; the Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast, the two-numbers framing in First Contact, and "recharge refunds, payment earns" are the load-bearing devices and all three land. /how-it-works is the weak link structurally — it is three pages wearing one URL, and both of my medium frictions live there, in the seam between the summary layer and the detail layer (the takeaway box understates the cost range; the tiny-deltas rule is contradicted in feel by a 0.15 penalty five sections later).

The one thing I'd fix first is the ceiling contradiction. The trust ceiling is the single number that determines what *my* participation could ever amount to, the site makes it central (Alice's whole arc is fenced by it, Risk 8's equity argument is built on it), and the two reference surfaces a visitor would check — the explainer table and the glossary — currently give different answers for the same phone. Everything else on this site taught me to trust its numbers; this is the place where two of them disagree.
