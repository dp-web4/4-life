## Unanswered Questions

1. **If I run Hestia today, what is my trust ceiling?** The page never says. It tells me the answer is elsewhere. I want the number at the point of decision.
2. **Software-only sits exactly at 0.50, which is exactly the rebirth threshold. Which side of the line am I on, and can I ever get off it?** If a software-only identity dips to 0.45, can it climb back to 0.50, or is the ceiling also a one-way ratchet?
3. **Why did Life 1 end with 145 ATP still in the tank?** Asked as a reader of the story, not as a reader of the spec.
4. **Who are the "independent reviewers" and the "witness panel" in the appeals process?** In a system whose whole premise is no central authority, someone still has to be the neutral party. Are they paid? Elected? Randomly drawn from high-trust members? The design lists the steps but not the staffing.
5. **Who vouches for a brand-new member who loses their only device in week one?** The 3-to-7-day recovery assumes a community I have not built yet.
6. **What does a 0.50 person actually experience day to day?** `/day-in-web4` is a day in the life of someone with a normal trust score. Given the site is admirably direct that ~3 billion people will be at the software tier, the more useful story might be a day in *their* internet. Can they post? Get answers? Sell something? Or is 0.50 quietly the floor of visibility?
7. **What does MCP have to do with any of this?** It is one fifth of the core equation and I finished the site without a working idea of its role.

## Honest Assessment

This site is unusually good at the thing most technical sites are worst at: it tells you what is not true yet. "Honest research doesn't ship deadlines," "the badge says how far a piece has travelled, not how good it is," "the policy gate stops accidents, not adversaries," "silence in it is not proof that nothing happened," and, most impressively, publishing the measured bypass of its own shipped safety feature ("two environment variables are enough to get past it"). I have read a lot of project sites. I have never read one that documented how to defeat its own gate. That single paragraph did more for my trust in the project than the whole hero section.

The teaching is also genuinely good. Web2 versus Web3 versus Web4 in three sentences. MRH as "hearing only conversations you are part of." Talent never decays, Temperament decays in a month, which is a claim about human nature I happen to agree with. And showing the arithmetic on the eligibility check instead of just asserting 0.52. I came in with zero background and left able to explain the core idea to someone else, which is the actual test.

Where it lost me is a single seam, and it is the same seam every time: **the gap between what the site sells and what the site runs.** The front door sells hardware-anchored identity. The one thing badged Running is not hardware-anchored. That is not dishonesty, because every individual page says the true thing about itself. It is a routing failure. The truth is distributed across `/hestia` (defers binding), `/lct-explainer` (0.50 ceiling), `/karma-consequences` (record stays sheddable) and `/what-could-go-wrong` (0.50 is where it stops), and no single page assembles it. I assembled it, over about twenty minutes, because I was stubborn. Most people are not stubborn. Most people read `/tldr`, see "Hestia: Running, on hardware you control," and form a belief that is wrong in the specific direction that flatters the project.

The fix is small and the site clearly has the appetite for it, because `/what-could-go-wrong` already contains every hard sentence needed. It just needs those sentences to arrive where the decisions are made rather than four clicks away. A project this willing to publish its own failure modes should not be leaving its best honesty in the basement.

Second smaller note: the site's promise to spell out every term on first use is kept everywhere except `/the-standard`, which opens with a five-term equation containing two terms the reading path never introduced. That page reads like it was written for a different audience than the rest of the site, and a curious non-technical visitor who follows the "See how it fits together" CTA lands there.

Would I return? Yes, mostly to see whether the hub ever gets a live network, because that is the experiment I actually want to see the result of. Would I recommend it? To a technical friend, yes, immediately. To a non-technical friend, maybe, with the warning to stop at `/how-it-works` and to read `/what-could-go-wrong` before believing anything.
