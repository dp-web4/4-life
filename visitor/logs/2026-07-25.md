## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)

**First impression**: Strong. "Trust, built into the internet." then immediately "A proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority, and by what rules." I knew what this site was about in about eight seconds, which is rare.

The three-beat opening works:
- "AI agents already take real actions: they write code, move money, and make decisions."
- "Today we cannot prove what an agent did, on whose authority, or by what rules."
- "Web4 closes that gap. Trust is earned through witnessed behavior, not declared by a platform and not assumed because someone holds a key."

And then a definition of the load-bearing word before I could get confused by it: "To witness is to observe an action and attest to it, staking your own reputation on the record." Good. That is the right sentence in the right place.

**Clicked**: The page told me where to go: "New to Web4? Read in this order: the 2-minute intro, Why Web4, then First Contact." I obeyed. That instruction is worth a lot; I did not have to guess.

**Confusion points**: The nav is very wide (Start Here / The Onramp / Core Concepts x9 / Going Deeper x8 = about 25 destinations). Seeing "Coherence Index", "Identity Constellation", "Manifest" in the menu before I knew what any of them were was mildly intimidating, but the "read in this order" line defused it. Minor.

### Web4 in 2 Minutes — /tldr (04:30)

Best page on the site. "Plain English first, so you can read this cold. Every term is spelled out the first time it appears, and nothing here needs a background in crypto or AI." It kept that promise.

The Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast landed instantly:
- Web2: "A platform *declares* your reputation."
- Web3: "Whoever holds the key *is assumed to be* the rightful owner."
- Web4: "Trust accrues from *witnessed behavior*."

That is the clearest thing I read all session. I understood the pitch from those three lines alone.

"How real is it, honestly?" is genuinely disarming. "Web4 is R&D, not a finished product. Here is the real state of each piece, with no polish on it... No timeline commitments. This is research, and research takes as long as it takes." I trusted the site more after reading that than before. The `pip install web4-core` / `cargo add web4-core` line is a good credibility anchor.

**Confusion point**: "The hub — Community scale: the program a group runs to become its own small Web4 society." I did not know what a "Web4 society" was, and the tldr never says. I carried that unresolved for the rest of the session; the glossary eventually answered it ("A self-governing collective of LCT-bearing entities with shared law, a witnessed ledger, and reified resources") but that definition is itself made of three terms I did not have yet, and I only found it at minute 50.

Also: "Hestia runs end to end today." End to end *what*? At this point in my reading, "end to end" had no referent. (The Hestia page answered it beautifully later, but the tldr sentence is doing nothing for me on first read.)

The badge key was handled well: "Reference — built and runnable (runnable is not the same as running in production)" and "Running — deployed and operational today, in day-to-day use," with a link to the full key. That parenthetical is exactly the right amount of pedantry.

### Why Web4? — /why-web4 (10:00)

The "problems you already know" section is persuasive and does not need me to understand anything technical. "Creating accounts is free. Sending messages is free. There's no cost to flooding platforms with garbage." Yes. "You've spent years building reputation on one platform. Then you try a new service: you're treated like a stranger." Yes, felt that.

"**Common thread:** All these solutions treat symptoms, not causes" is a good pivot.

The FAQ is where this page earns its keep. "Research prototype with substantial implementation. This is not vaporware - there is real, tested code." "Isn't this just a social credit system? Fair question." Answering the hostile question in the hostile question's own words is the right instinct.

**Confusion point (the one that stuck)**: hardware. This page told me:
- "Most phones sold since ~2018 include security chips... The threshold is a $50 phone, not a $1000 one."
- "USB-based keys like YubiKey cost ~$25 and work with any computer."
- "The most marginalized populations - those without any device access - would need some form of sponsored onboarding."

The framing throughout is *how cheap the hardware is*, which strongly implied to me: **you need the hardware.** I closed this page believing Web4 was gated on owning a security chip. That belief turned out to be wrong, and I only found out four pages later.

### First Contact — /first-contact (17:00)

I liked the setup a lot: "Only two numbers move while she acts. This is all you need to follow the story." Reducing a five-acronym system to two tracked numbers for a first-timer is a smart call.

"🔋 Energy Budget (ATP) — the resource Alice spends to act. Starts at 100... 🤝 Trust Score — her *raw* reputation, on a 0.00-1.00 scale. Starts neutral at 0.50."

The five acts (Join / Build / Stumble / Recover / Die & Reborn) are a good arc. And this line saved me from real alarm:

> "Spoiler, so you're not left hanging: 'death' here means Alice's *standing* is suspended - she can't act until rebirth - not that her account is deleted."

Good. I relaxed. Then I read the next page and un-relaxed. See friction #1.

### How It Works — /how-it-works (23:00)

This is where the session got hard.

The content is good. The five systems in plain English are genuinely well written, especially CI: "If someone usually posts coding tutorials at 9am and suddenly starts posting crypto spam at 3am from a new country, their coherence drops - making every action more expensive." That is a concept I now own.

"Lurking is always free. You only spend energy when you act" was a relief; I had been quietly worried I'd be metered for reading.

The plagiarism walkthrough (Dr. Chen, the 40% overlap, the appeal, the rotating moderator, the two outcomes) is the single most convincing thing on the site. It made an abstract governance framework feel like something that could actually happen to a person. And it closes honestly: "the appeals mechanism is formally specified (109 integration checks) but hasn't been tested with real humans yet."

**But**: I counted 25 headings on this page. It runs from "Birth" through federation economics, four trust invariants, correlated-equilibrium game theory, SAL governance, a plagiarism case, agent-chain trust decay, and a "what about multiple communities" coda. This is not a page, it is a book. Around heading 14 ("How The Pieces Fit Together") I started skimming, and I know I skimmed past things I'd have valued. There is no table of contents, no "read the first three sections and stop" guidance, and no reading-time estimate — which is odd, because /why-web4 does exactly that ("three parts: the problems (~5 min), what Web4 proposes (~2 min), and honest Q&A").

**And the contradiction**: this page opens its death section with

> "Death in Web4 is not a timeout or suspension. Your energy budget is depleted - you can no longer act."

I had read the *opposite* sentence twenty minutes earlier on First Contact ("'death' here means Alice's standing is *suspended*"). I went to /atp-economics to break the tie and it sided with First Contact: "closer to a suspended license reinstated than a clean slate."

So two pages say death is a suspension and one page says it is explicitly not one. I do not know which is true. As a naive reader my honest conclusion was "they have not agreed on this internally," which is a worse impression than either answer would have been.

Also on this page: "the canonical composite weights (talent 0.4 / training 0.3 / temperament 0.3)". Hold that thought.

### ATP Economics — /atp-economics (31:00)

"Think of ATP as cash and the ADP as the receipt (automatic - the system keeps it in the background, you never hold or manage it)." Good — "you never hold or manage it" preempted my next question.

"This is not a free reset like abandoning a spam account and signing up fresh: your identity and full history persist." That is the actual point of the death mechanic and it is stated well here.

But three sentences on this same page still say "run out and your agent dies" / "Run out of energy budget? You die." The word "die" is doing the visitor no favours anywhere on this site. It is the most memorable and most misleading word in the vocabulary, and every page has to spend a sentence walking it back.

### Running Now — /running-now (36:00)

Excellent page, and the one I'd send a skeptic to first. The badge legend is unambiguous:
- "Spec — defined in the Web4 standard. Written down and stable, not yet built here."
- "Reference — built and runnable. Finished code you could install and start today."
- "Running — deployed and operational today. Live instances actually exist and are in day-to-day use."

Concrete numbers (`web4-core` v0.3.0, 171 passing tests, 2,627 in the Python SDK, a 6 MB Rust binary) do more for credibility than any amount of prose.

"Where you fit today" is the best navigational device on the site. Five reader categories, each with one honest sentence and one link:
- "You are here to understand it, not to run it."
- "You run AI agents on your own machine and are comfortable at a command line. Hestia is the piece for you, and it is the one already in day-to-day use."
- "You have a group that wants to govern itself."
- "You are building on the primitives."
- "Your organization has to answer to auditors."

I found myself in category 2 in about four seconds. **This is the routing the rest of the site needs and does not have.** More on that under /onramp.

And the honest bottom line names something no other page did: "Federation across societies remains Spec only, specified but not yet built." Given how much /how-it-works leans on federation (exit rights, forking to a competitor society, trust portability, "if the Open Science Collective becomes authoritarian, members migrate"), learning here that federation *does not exist yet* retroactively weakened several of the governance arguments I had already accepted. /how-it-works presents those as the answer to "what stops unfair rules" without noting anywhere that the mechanism is unbuilt.

I came here from the landing page's second CTA, "See how it fits together." It is a decent structural page — "One substrate, three scales", dependency direction, where the pieces connect — and the R6/R7 gloss is nicely done ("the standard six-part shape of any action, so a request like 'post this' or 'spend 5 ATP' gets checked and trust-scored the same way every time").

That is not adoption guidance, that is a restatement of the page's own diagram. I *am* the person asking "what do I pick up first," and I got told to pick. The onramp page is one of the two things the landing page offers me and it hands off worse than the page I found by accident.

### LCT Explainer — /lct-explainer (45:00)

"Imagine your phone becoming your driver's license - but one that can't be photocopied, faked from a stolen photo, or impersonated from a remote computer." Best analogy on the site. The Linked / Context / Token breakdown is clean, and "a cryptographic key held by the user that signs actions, not a server-lookup credential" is a real distinction I now understand.

Then: **"Only have one device? You're still in." "Single-device users aren't excluded — they just have a lower trust ceiling." "You can fully participate, post, earn ATP, and build karma."**

This directly reversed the belief /why-web4 had given me. Good news, but I had been carrying the wrong model for thirty minutes, and I only corrected it because I happened to click a concept page. The correction lives on a page a casual reader may never reach.

The ceiling table:

| Tier | Hardware | Max |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest | TPM Chip | 0.90 |
| Strong | Secure Enclave | 0.85 |
| Good | FIDO2 Key | 0.75 |
| Basic | Software Only | 0.50 |

with "with software-only hardware, 0.5 is both where you start and the highest you can reach."

I stared at that for a while, because I had already read, on /how-it-works: **"Aliveness: ATP > 0 + Trust > 0.5 + CI coherent = alive."** Strictly greater than 0.5.

So: software-only tops out *at* 0.5. Aliveness requires trust *above* 0.5. Rebirth eligibility is "Overall T3 score ≥ 0.5" and "Not Eligible: Overall T3 score < 0.5. No rebirth. Permanent death." /what-could-go-wrong states it flatly: "Fall below it and you're out."

Read together, the site tells me a software-only user starts at their permanent ceiling, sits exactly on the survival line forever, can never rise above it, and is one rounding decision away from ineligible. That is not what "You're still in. You can fully participate" describes. One of these two things is wrong and I cannot tell which. This is the single sharpest thing I hit all session.

### Trust Tensor — /trust-tensor (49:00)

"Web4 doesn't reduce trust to a single number." Clear. Talent / Training / Temperament, always role-scoped, is a good idea well explained, and "0.5 = newcomer baseline, 0.7 = ~70% cooperative behavior in this role, 0.9 = consistently exceptional" is the kind of calibration sentence I wish more of the site had. "That same 0.5 is what other pages call the survival line" is a nice cross-page stitch.

**Small snag**: this page shows Talent 40% / Training 35% / Temperament 25%, labelled as the Data Analyst role. /how-it-works showed "the canonical composite weights (talent 0.4 / training 0.3 / temperament 0.3)." I *think* the first is role-specific and the second is the default, but nothing on either page says that, and the word "canonical" on one of them made me assume I'd caught an error rather than a role variation.

### Hestia — /hestia (52:00)

The best-written page on the site, full stop. "A day with Hestia" is six numbered steps and every one of them is a thing a person does:

> "You stash the secrets your agent will need (an API key, a repo token) in the vault. They sit encrypted on your disk, not pasted into a config file the agent can read wholesale."

> "Not a shared password you can never take back: a delegation you hand over on purpose."

> "Something looks off, so you revoke... The authority is gone immediately, and the tamper-evident record of what already happened stays."

That last one is the moment the whole framework clicked for me. Not the ontology, not the five systems — the fact that I can hand an agent 24 hours of scoped authority, watch what it does land in a chain as it happens, and yank it back without erasing the record. `hestia delegate grant my-agent --role administrator --expires 24` did more explaining than /how-it-works' 25 headings.

"No account on someone's server, no profile in a database, no cloud" answers the question I would have asked next.

If I were rebuilding the site I would put this page much earlier. It is the only place where the *value* of Web4 is shown rather than argued.

### What Could Go Wrong — /what-could-go-wrong (56:00)

Publishing your own eight worst failure modes with likelihood ratings is a real trust move and I respect it. "Nobody adopts it — Existential · High likelihood" as risk #1 is not what a project trying to sell me something would write.

Risk #8 is notable: "Users who cannot use biometrics... get pushed onto the software-only fallback at a 0.50 trust ceiling... Designing a hardware-anchored trust system that does not structurally disadvantage users with disabilities is an unsolved problem, not a solved one."

So the site *does* know the 0.50 ceiling is a real exclusion. That makes /lct-explainer's cheerful "You're still in. You can fully participate" read as the outlier rather than the truth. Which sharpens rather than resolves my confusion.

Risk #4's honesty is almost comic and I mean that as praise: "honest strategies yield ROI of ~0.93 while Sybil strategies yield ~0.90." That gap is *tiny*, and the page prints it anyway.

**"Aliveness" and "death" are not in the glossary.** These are the site's most distinctive, most alarming, and most internally-contradicted words. "Synthon" is there. "Federation" is there. The word that made me stop reading twice is not.

## Unanswered Questions

1. **Can a software-only user actually be alive?** Ceiling 0.50, aliveness requires >0.5. I genuinely cannot tell whether "you're still in" or "fall below it and you're out" is the operative rule for me.
2. **Is death a suspension or not?** Two pages say yes, one says explicitly no.
3. **Federation is Spec-only — so what actually protects me from an unfair society today?** Exit rights and "fork to a competitor" were the answers I was given, and both depend on trust portability that has not been built.
4. **Who runs the law oracle, and what is it?** It flags 40% overlap, classifies severity, produces verdicts. Software? A person? A model? It makes consequential judgments and I never learned what it *is*.
5. **How does a recipient "confirm value" in practice?** VCM is described as automatic-like-a-tip, but somebody has to press something. What is the actual gesture, and what stops a group from confirming each other's garbage?
6. **If I install Hestia today, who witnesses me?** The multi-device constellation is my own laptop and phone. Where do *other* people's attestations come from when there is no live network?
7. **What is my trust score at the start, and is 0.5 good or bad?** `/trust-tensor` says 0.5 is the newcomer baseline. `/how-it-works` says survival requires >0.5. So every newcomer starts one hair below alive. Is that intentional?
8. **"Reified resources"** — I looked up "society" to get *less* confused.

## Honest Assessment

This site is better than it was entitled to be. The first ten minutes are genuinely excellent: the landing page states the problem in three sentences, defines its one load-bearing verb before using it, and then tells me what order to read in — and `/tldr` cashes that promise with the clearest three-line framing of Web2/Web3/Web4 I could ask for. I have read a lot of project sites that never get to "here is what this is" at all. This one got there in eight seconds and then, remarkably, told me what does *not* work: "No timeline commitments. This is research, and research takes as long as it takes." I trusted the site more after that paragraph than before it, which is the opposite of how marketing usually goes.

The honesty is not decorative, either. `/what-could-go-wrong` lists "Nobody adopts it — Existential · High likelihood" as risk #1 and prints a simulation result where the honest strategy beats the cheating strategy by 0.03. Nobody fakes numbers that unflattering.

And `/hestia` is where I actually got converted, in the small way a naive visitor gets converted. Not by the ontology and not by the five interlocking systems — by six numbered steps in which I hand an agent 24 hours of scoped authority, watch its actions land in a chain as they happen, and revoke it when something looks off while the record of what it already did stays put. That is a thing I want, today, whether or not any of the rest of this arrives. If the site has a conversion moment, that is it, and it is buried at depth three.

What is holding it back is not writing quality. It is that the pages have not been read *against each other*. I hit two flat contradictions and neither required cleverness to find — just reading two pages in a row. First Contact told me "death means suspended," and twenty minutes later How It Works opened with "Death in Web4 is not a timeout or suspension." That is not a nuance I failed to grasp; that is two sentences that cannot both be true. Same with hardware: `/why-web4` spent its whole hardware FAQ persuading me the chip is affordable, which taught me the chip was mandatory, and `/lct-explainer` cheerfully told me four pages later that it isn't. Each page is individually well written. Read in sequence, they teach me a thing and then untech it.

The 0.50 one is worse than a contradiction because it is arithmetic. Software-only tops out at 0.50. Aliveness is "Trust > 0.5." Rebirth eligibility is "≥ 0.5," and below it is "permanent death." I did not need Web4 knowledge to see the problem; I needed a number line. And the site clearly knows something is wrong there, because `/what-could-go-wrong` #8 admits the software-only ceiling structurally disadvantages users who cannot use biometrics and calls it "an unsolved problem, not a solved one" — while `/lct-explainer` says "Only have one device? You're still in." Those two sentences are about the same people. When a project is this committed to honesty everywhere else, an unresolved seam like this reads as an oversight rather than a dodge, which is exactly why it is worth fixing: it is the one place where the site's carefulness visibly lapses.

`/how-it-works` is the other structural problem, and it is a problem of generosity rather than neglect. Twenty-five headings, every one of them containing something I would have wanted, and I skimmed the back half anyway. The Dr. Chen plagiarism walkthrough — detection, verdict, appeal, independent review, both possible outcomes, plus "hasn't been tested with real humans yet" — is the most convincing governance writing on the site, and it sits at roughly heading 19, past where I had already started scrolling. `/why-web4` labels its own sections with reading times. This page, which needs it four times as much, does not.

The routing failure is the same shape. `/running-now`'s "Where you fit today" put me in a bucket in four seconds and told me exactly which piece was mine. It is the best navigational thing on the site, and I found it by accident, on a page named for a different question. Meanwhile the section literally titled "Adoption order (what to pick up first)" on `/onramp` — the page the landing CTA sends me to — answers with "Start with the vocabulary, then pick your scale." I *was* the person asking what to pick up first, and I got told to pick.

None of this is far from fixed. A pass that reads the pages consecutively rather than individually would catch the death contradiction, the hardware whiplash, and the 0.50 arithmetic in an afternoon. Move the Hestia day-in-the-life earlier, lift "Where you fit today" into the onramp, and put a reading-time header on How It Works, and a naive visitor would leave with the same understanding I did in half the time and without the two moments where I stopped believing the site knew its own answer.
