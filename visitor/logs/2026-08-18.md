### Web4 in 2 Minutes (03:20)
- The opening line is a promise the page keeps: "Plain English first, so you can read this cold." It was true. Nothing here needed background.
- The Web2 / Web3 / Web4 three-column comparison is the single most efficient teaching device on the site. Web2 = platform declares your reputation and can delete it. Web3 = whoever holds the key is assumed to be you. Web4 = other participants observe what you do and sign a small record of it. I got the core idea from that one row.
- The Spec / Reference / Running badge definitions are spelled out right where the badges first appear. That was a good decision. I never had to guess what a badge meant on any later page.
- Small thing I noticed: the badge legend defines **Spec** as "defined in the standard, not yet built as reference code," but none of the four pieces on this page carries a Spec badge. I spent a few seconds looking for one before moving on.
- Confusion points: "witnessing" is introduced as observing an action and "staking your reputation" on it. I understood the words but not the stakes. What actually happens to a witness who signs off on something that turns out to be false? The page does not say, and I kept wanting that answer for the rest of the session.
- Clicked: **Why Web4?** (the page told me to, and I trusted it by this point).

### Why Web4? (07:45)
- The problem framing is strong and does not require me to already believe in the solution. Spam is free, reputation is trapped per-platform, bans cost nothing to evade, and you cannot tell a human from an agent. All four are things I have personally experienced.
- "Why previous solutions failed" was the section that made me take this seriously. It names moderation armies, CAPTCHAs, Web3 wallets, and social login, and gives each one a one-line kill shot. "AI solves CAPTCHAs now." Fine, yes.
- The FAQ is enormous. The index summary says "Browse all 72 questions by topic" and I did the arithmetic out of skepticism: 12 + 13 + 11 + 14 + 8 + 6 + 8 = 72. It adds up exactly. That is a small thing but it made me trust the rest of the page's numbers more, which turned out to matter.
- The honest scoreboard near the bottom (checkmarks for spec, reference implementations, security validation, hardware integration; crosses for production deployment, economic validation, platform adoption) is exactly what I want from a research project. No page I have ever read about a protocol has volunteered its own crosses this readably.
- Confusion points: this page fires a lot of numbers at me at once. 0.5, 3 hops, 0.7 per-hop decay, 0.02 update step, 5% transfer fee, 424 attack vectors, 85% detection, 55 tests, 93% collusion detection, 180-day and 30-day half-lives. Individually each has a home. As a first-time reader I could not hold them, and I could not tell which ones are load-bearing design constants and which ones are validation trivia.
- Clicked: **First Contact**.

### First Contact (14:30)
- The setup is good: two metrics to watch (ATP energy, trust score), a five-act arc, an explicit promise that I drive the choices.
- The honesty here is unusually careful. "The rules running here are real; Web4 itself is still active research, with no live network to join yet." And later: "you're following a guided walkthrough of the proposed model, not using a product." I never once thought I was signing up for something.
- I liked the "raw vs effective" note and the callout that the walkthrough follows a **hardware-anchored** identity and that "Alice's climb is not the shape every tier sees." That is the site pre-empting a criticism instead of waiting for me to make it.
- **Where I got stuck**: the act summary cards give outcomes as bare numbers. Act 2 up to 0.56, Act 3 down to 0.48, Act 4 up to 0.62, Act 5 "dies at ATP 0, trust 0.51; +12 bonus; reborn at 0.54". Alice ends Act 4 at 0.62 and enters death in Act 5 at 0.51. Eleven points of trust vanish between the two cards and the summary never says why. I stared at that for a while assuming I had misread it. Maybe the interactive player explains it during Act 5, but the card I could see does not, and the cards are what a skimmer reads.
- Also: the page has spent paragraphs teaching me that 0.50 is the survival line, and then Alice dies at **0.51**, above it. It turns out she died of energy, not trust, but I had to work that out myself from a later page. At the moment I read the card, "dies at trust 0.51" read like the threshold had been violated.
- Clicked: **How It Works**.

### How It Works (23:10)
- This is the densest page and mostly earns it. Five foundational systems, then a birth / life / death / rebirth lifecycle, then feedback loops, then governance, then chained agents, then federation.
- The trust math is actually shown rather than asserted: `base_delta = 0.02 × (quality − 0.5)`, scaled talent ×1.0, training ×0.8, temperament ×0.6, composite weights 0.4 / 0.3 / 0.3. A quality-0.85 contribution gives +0.007 to talent. Seeing how small that is taught me more about the design philosophy than any prose paragraph did. Trust is deliberately slow. Got it.
- The two-deaths distinction (energy death recoverable, trust death permanent) is clean and it retroactively fixed my First Contact confusion.
- The chained-agent number checks out: 0.9 to the fifth is about 0.59, and the page explicitly says the 90% kept per step is that agent's own trust and not a hop fee. Good pre-emption of exactly the wrong inference I was about to make.
- **Biggest friction of the session**: the page's one complete worked example is a three-life progression. Life 1 born with 100, ends with 145. Life 2 reborn with 145, ends with 130. Life 3 reborn with 130, ends with 165. Trust climbs 0.65, 0.72, 0.85 throughout. And then the page tells me, in a note titled "About these numbers", that none of these lives is an energy death and none is a trust death, "that is why they read *ended* and not *died*", and that what else ends a life is not settled.
  - I want to be fair: that disclosure is more honest than most projects would be, and I noticed it.
  - But the effect on me as a reader was that the flagship end-to-end example of a page called "The Complete Lifecycle" turns on an event the page cannot name. Alice's three lives end for no stated reason. I finished the lifecycle page not knowing what ends a life, having just read three of them end.
- Confusion points: the federation section is written in confident present tense ("ATP prices adjust dynamically based on supply and demand", "the system detects policy conflicts"). The page does carry a global caveat that present tense describes model behavior. On `/running-now` I later read "Federation across societies remains Spec only, specified but not yet built." Those two readings sit far enough apart that I believed the first one for ten minutes.

### LCT Explainer (42:00)
- The ceilings are laid out per anchoring tier: TPM chip max 0.90, Secure Enclave max 0.85, FIDO2 key max 0.75, software only max 0.50.

The answer is that trust death is sustained collapse, not a snapshot, so a software-only identity is not dead, it just has "no buffer above it, so any dip restricts your features until you climb back."

- Went hunting for the five tiers: not in the glossary either. Eventually found them inside a collapsed FAQ answer on `/why-web4`: Wrapper, Observable, Accountable, Federated, Native. Fine definitions, but they are three clicks and a search away from the page that referenced them.

## Honest Assessment

This site is unusually good at the thing most projects are worst at, and slightly weak at the thing most projects find easy.

The thing it is good at: telling me what is not true yet. I have never read a technical site that voluntarily shrinks its own headline number. `/running-now` says 171 tests and then explains that the impressive 2,627 figure belongs to a different artifact. It says hestia is in daily use and then says the four machines are all the lab's own and that this "does not claim adoption", and then it tells me where to go verify who the lab is. It tells me the witness chain is a record of governed activity and not of all activity, and that two environment variables get past the gate, and that this is an accepted limit and not a scheduled repair. `/onramp` tells me there is no hub-to-hardbound integration at all. `/what-could-go-wrong` opens with "nobody adopts it, existential, high." By the end I trusted the site's factual claims more than I trust most documentation, and that trust was earned by subtraction rather than assertion.

The thing it is weaker at: the worked examples do not always obey the rules the prose just taught me. This showed up three times and it was always the same shape. Alice as Mechanic scores 0.28 on a page that never mentions the 0.5 survival line, while two other pages call sustained sub-0.5 permanent death. The three-life progression on the lifecycle page has three lives end without any of the page's two death conditions being met, which the page admits in a footnote after the fact. Alice in First Contact loses eleven points of trust between two summary cards with no stated cause and then "dies" at 0.51 on a page that spent three paragraphs establishing 0.50 as the line. Individually each of these is small. Together they mean the illustrations, which are the parts a naive reader leans on hardest, are the least reliable part of the site. I kept catching the prose being careful and the numbers in the examples being loose.

There is also a mild structural thing. The five-page reading path is well ordered and well timed, and I followed it exactly as instructed, and it left two of the six landing-page primitives underexplained. ADP is half of a nav label that the whole path never expands. V3 gets one clause. Both are one sentence away from fixed.

What I would tell a friend: read `/tldr`, then `/running-now`, then `/what-could-go-wrong`. That is a fifteen-minute path and it is the most honest fifteen minutes about an early-stage protocol that I have read. Then read the rest if you are still curious, and treat the numbers inside the worked examples as illustrative rather than as rules, because in at least three places the site itself agrees they are.

Would I come back? Yes, and specifically to find out whether anyone ever stands up a hub instance. The site is careful to say that no one has yet, and that "pilot could start whenever a willing community partners with the research." That is a falsifiable claim with a date attached to it in the future, which is more than most sites in this space offer, and it is the reason I would check again.
