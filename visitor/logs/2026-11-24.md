## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level): a proposed way to make trust come from witnessed behaviour instead of a platform's say-so or possession of a key. Taught by /tldr and /why-web4.
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together: one shared vocabulary plus three sizes (person, community, enterprise). Taught clearly by /tldr and /onramp.
- [x] What LCT means: Linked Context Token, an identity that lives on my device, ideally in its security chip, vouched for by my other devices. Taught, though I could not tell you what one actually looks like.
- [ ] What ATP/ADP means: I get the idea (an energy budget; spending leaves a receipt; other people confirming value refills it; real gain comes from commissioned work). I do not trust any number attached to it, and I cannot tell whether supply is capped. Half taught.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are: three scores (Talent, Training, Temperament) per role. Taught very well by the surgeon example.
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D: the site's strongest suit. One real exception: I cannot tell whether a hub is running anywhere.
- [x] Why any of this matters: yes, for AI agents especially. /why-web4's problem section did this.

## Honest Assessment
There are two sites here and they are not equally good.

The first is the *status* site: /tldr, /running-now, /what-could-go-wrong, the maturity sections on each onramp page. It is some of the most honest writing about a software project I have come across. It tells me the only daily users are the builders, that the safety gate can be bypassed with two environment variables, that the hardware binding has never been checked on a real device, that software-only users are stuck at the bottom tier and that this falls hardest on people with the least. I finished those pages believing the authors, which is presumably the point of a project about trust.

The second is the *mechanics* site: /how-it-works, /atp-economics, /karma-consequences, /coherence-index, /day-in-web4. Here the same honesty turns into a different problem. Nearly every worked example carries a note saying the numbers are illustrative, unsettled, "one modelling choice", "this site's calibration, not the standard's", or contradicted on another page. Individually each note is admirable. Together they mean I cannot check anything: the quality table does not follow its own rule, the karma for the same death is +12 here and -15 there, CI has a death line on its own page and explicitly does not on three others, and whether strangers can see my posts depends on which paragraph I read. A naive reader does not come away thinking "this is early"; they come away thinking "these people cannot agree with themselves", which is a worse impression than the project deserves given how careful the status pages are.

If I were advising: show me fewer numbers and make the ones you show agree. I did not need eleven distinct thresholds to understand the idea. The surgeon who should not fix my car, the mail that costs the sender energy, the phone stolen in Madrid: those taught me the system. The tables mostly un-taught it.

The reading order also worked against me. The official five-page path never visits LCT, trust tensors or A Day in Web4, yet ends on a page that says it assumes that vocabulary. A Day in Web4 was the first page where I could picture using any of this, and I found it twentieth.

Finally, the thing I keep turning over: the one piece that actually runs gives its users an identity capped at exactly the score they start with, which is also the score they must not fall below. I learned that from a caveat. If that is the real state of things today, it deserves to be said in the first paragraph of the hestia page, because it changes what "Running" means for anyone who is not the lab.

Would I come back? If I were building with AI agents, yes, for hestia and the status pages. As a curious passer-by, I got the idea in the first fifteen minutes and the following eighty mostly cost me confidence rather than adding understanding.
