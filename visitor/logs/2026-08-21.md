## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level) — trust earned from witnessed behaviour rather than declared by a platform or assumed from key possession. The Web2/Web3/Web4 triptych on `/tldr` did this in three sentences.

- [x] What LCT means — Linked Context Token, a device-anchored identity that signs actions. The word-by-word breakdown (Linked / Context / Token) is what made the name stop being noise.
- [x] What ATP/ADP means — Allocation Transfer Packets / Allocation Discharge Packets. Spend ATP to act, the ADP receipt records it, recipients confirming quality converts it back. I also understood that supply is set by each society, not by the protocol.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are — three role-scoped dimensions, Talent / Training / Temperament, weighted 0.4 / 0.3 / 0.3 into a composite. (Learned from `/trust-tensor` and `/how-it-works`. `/first-contact` actively taught me a wrong version of this — see HIGH 1.)
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D — unusually clearly. Spec / Reference / Running, with "Running" explicitly disclaiming adoption, and `/running-now` naming whose four machines those are.
- [x] Why any of this matters — yes, and `/what-could-go-wrong` is a large part of why I believe it.

## Unanswered Questions
1. **What actually happens to my trust score when I die and come back?** The site tells me twice, in opposite directions (−5% on `/karma-consequences`, +0.03 on `/first-contact`), and fences neither.
2. **How many actions is a "life"?** I now know a single action moves composite trust by roughly ±0.006. Alice goes 0.50 → 0.62 in five acts; `/how-it-works`'s Novice goes 0.50 → 0.65 across "dozen+ actions" (which by the rule needs more like twenty-five). I could not work out whether an act is one action, ten, or a month.
3. **Who commissions a newcomer's first piece of work?** `/how-it-works` says commissioned work is the only thing that nets positive ATP, self-initiated work refunds at best, and then says *"How someone with no track record lands a first commission is an open question on this stack rather than a solved one."* I appreciate the honesty, but this looks load-bearing: if nobody commissions newcomers, every newcomer's balance only goes down. `/day-in-web4` shows a first post earning 15 ATP back on a 10 ATP spend, which is the *self-initiated* case netting positive — the case `/how-it-works` says caps at zero. I could not tell which is right.
4. **What is the actual ceiling on a normal person?** `/lct-explainer` gives chip tiers (TPM 0.90, Secure Enclave 0.85, FIDO2 0.75, software 0.50) *and* device counts (1 device 0.50–0.75, 2 devices 0.75, 3+ up to 0.90), and says the lower of the two rules wins. So a one-phone person is capped at 0.75 forever unless they buy more devices. Is 0.75 enough to do anything meaningful? The page tells me what the number is but not what it buys.
5. **Who assigns the quality score?** `/trust-tensor` says the recipient does, via V3. `/why-web4` FAQ #8 asks *"Who decides what counts as 'quality'?"*. I read both and still cannot picture the moment — is it a thumbs-up, a form, a rating? `/first-contact` says "a simple thumbs-up", `/value-tensor` implies three separate scored dimensions. Those feel like different amounts of work to ask of a person.

## Honest Assessment

This site is much better than it was entitled to be, and its best quality is a strange one: **it argues against itself in writing, on purpose, and it is usually right when it does.**

The pattern showed up four separate times. I would form an objection, get halfway to writing it down, and find the site had already made it. `/how-it-works` notices that its own three-life example has lives ending at 145 ATP when death is defined as ATP reaching zero, says so, and then *refuses to invent a cause* — "rather than invent a third cause to justify these figures, this page declines to name one." `/lct-explainer` has my software-only-ceiling objection as a literal heading. `/tldr` withdraws the strongest reading of its own "Running" badge in the same breath as issuing it. `/what-could-go-wrong` grades two of its own failure modes as undetectable by any mechanism. I have not read a project site that does this. It bought a large amount of credibility, and it bought it cheaply, because all it costs is saying the true thing.

Which is exactly why the three high-severity findings sting. **All three sit on `/first-contact` — the one page the reading path treats as the emotional core, the one I would have called the best-crafted page on the site before I checked its arithmetic.** The gap is not carelessness in general; it is that the discipline applied everywhere else was not applied to the walkthrough. `/how-it-works` fences its own karma numbers three ways and reconciles its own trust climb ("dozen+ actions") — and then points at First Contact and says *"same rules, same numbers"* without checking. `/karma-consequences` fences the ATP formula in a whole paragraph and leaves the trust formula sitting unfenced one line below it, contradicting Alice directly. And the T3 dimensions are simply wrong in one bullet, in a form no page-level grep would ever find, because it renders only in the client bundle after you click to Act 5.

There is a theme worth naming: **the site's prose is far more careful than its illustrations.** Every contradiction I found is a number or a label inside a demo — Alice's trust deltas, the 0.91 community average, the Face ID step, the "everyone starts equal" caption. The essays are audited; the story panels and the mockups are not. If I were prioritising, I would point the same scrutiny that produced that "About these numbers" paragraph at every literal value the site renders, and I would start with the walkthrough, because that is the page a newcomer is told to trust before they have any way to check it.

Two smaller things. The 30-minute promise is wrong by half, and it is wrong on the one page where being wrong costs the most — page four, right when I was deciding whether to finish. I would rather be told 45. And the density curve is inverted at the very top: the landing page's Hub card is harder reading than `/tldr`'s version of the same card, which is one click deeper.

I would come back. I would send this to a sceptical friend and tell them to start at `/tldr` and read `/what-could-go-wrong` second — because the honesty is the strongest argument the project has, and right now it is buried on page nine.
