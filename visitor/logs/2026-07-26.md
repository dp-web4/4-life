### Why Web4? (/why-web4) (09:00)
- It tells you its own shape up front: three parts, ~5 min / ~2 min / optional Q&A, "Stop reading at any point - each section stands on its own." And it offers two escape hatches: the 2-minute version if I have less time, or First Contact if I would rather meet the acronyms inside a story. I have never seen a long page manage my time that considerately.
- The five problems (spam is free, reputation is siloed, bad actors get fresh starts, AI makes it worse, platforms control everything) each end with a **Root cause:** line. Problem before solution, exactly.
- "Why Previous Solutions Failed" gives each failed approach a **What it gets right** before its **Failure**. Web3 gets credit for genuine self-sovereignty; social login gets credit for usability. Steelmanning the alternatives made me trust the argument more than if it had dunked on them.
- The four mechanisms are each introduced in plain English *first*, with the acronym demoted to a "Shorthand:" line afterward. "MRH (Markov Relevancy Horizon - math-flavored jargon you can ignore; it just means how far trust reaches before it fades)." Telling me I can ignore the scary name is disarming and it worked.
- "All four, in one moment" — the forum-reply vignette where all four mechanisms fire on one tap of *reply* — is where the system actually clicked into a whole for me. Before it, I had four facts. After it, I had one machine.
- The honest Q&A is enormous (60+ questions) but it labels itself "reference depth, not a read-through" and gives a topic index. I skimmed ~15 answers. "Isn't this just a social credit system?" is answered with a comparison table *and* a caveat admitting a Web4 society could be configured oppressively and that the only real safeguard is the exit right. That caveat is what made me believe the rest of the page.
- Confusion points: one small integrity nit in the topic index (see friction log). Nothing conceptual.

### Trust Neighborhood (/trust-neighborhood) (21:00)
- Went here because /why-web4 gave me hop weights (0.70 / 0.49 / 0.34) and I wanted to see if the dedicated page agreed. **It did** — same numbers, plus the formula `trust = t₁ × t₂ × t₃ × 0.7^depth`. Two pages, same quantity, same value. I checked specifically because sites usually fail this, and it passed.
- Better: it explains *why* 0.7 and not something else — 0.5 is too harsh (friends-of-friends-of-friends go invisible), 0.9 is too loose (the boundary stops existing) — and then says outright "0.7 is a design parameter, not a physical constant. Different communities might tune it." Showing me the reasoning behind a number, and admitting it is a choice, is the opposite of hand-waving.
- The party analogy (you can be introduced across the room but not walk up to a stranger) did more work for me than the percentages did.

### Running Now (/running-now) (27:00)
- Opening line: "Web4 is R&D, not a shipped product. Nothing on this page is a fielded production system, and this page will not pretend otherwise." Good.
- The three-level badge key is precise in a way I respect: **Spec** = written down, not built here. **Reference** = "Finished code you could install and start today. Runnable is not the same as running in production." **Running** = "deployed and operational today. Live instances actually exist and are in day-to-day use."
- "Where you fit today" routes by *what I want to do* rather than by product name — understand without running / run agents on my own machine / group governance / build on primitives / answer to auditors. That is the right axis. It is the section that told me what my actual next step was.

Under Hestia: "Hardware binding is trait contracts only for now, deferred to the hardbound tier below." Hold that thought.

### LCT / Verified Presence (/lct-explainer) (38:00)
- The chip explanation is genuinely good for a layperson: the private key "is generated inside the silicon and physically can't leave it: not even the operating system or malware can read it out." Comparing it to what already protects Face ID gave me a real-world anchor.
- Trust ceilings by hardware type — TPM 0.90, Secure Enclave 0.85, FIDO2 0.75, software-only 0.50 — with "Only have one device? You're still in." I had been quietly worried this was a rich-person's protocol. It answered that without my asking.
- **And then the contradiction.** This page says: "Web4's TPM2 binding has been validated on real hardware (Intel TPM 2.0: key creation, signing, verification, attestation quotes, EK certificate chain verified through 2049)," and, being careful, distinguishes layers: "the _hardware layer_ - the chip-level identity binding - really is tested on silicon. The trust, witness, and economic dynamics built on top of it are still simulation."
- But /running-now told me the hardware binding is the part that is **not** validated — "CI runs against mocks." So one page tells me hardware is the proven foundation and simulation is the soft part, and the other tells me the hardware binding specifically is the mocked part. Those cannot both be my takeaway. Worse, this page *links me to /running-now* mid-sentence, so the site walked me directly into the disagreement.
- I suspect the reconciliation is that two different codebases are being described (the core standard's TPM work on an Intel chip vs. hardbound's binding on its Jetson target), but the site never says that. I had to invent that explanation myself, and I might be wrong.

### Hestia (/hestia) (51:00)
- "A day with Hestia" is the most concrete thing on the entire site: six numbered steps with real commands (`hestia init`, `hestia vault add`, `hestia delegate grant my-agent --role administrator --expires 24`, `hestia delegate revoke`, `hestia connect-hub <url>`, `hestia serve`). After 50 minutes of concepts, watching someone grant an agent 24-hour scoped authority and then revoke it — with the prior actions still tamper-evident afterward — is what made "witnessed trust record" stop being a phrase.
- "Fails closed if asked to serve off-loopback" is a detail that signals the authors care about the boring parts.
- Confusion points: this page describes itself as "Phase 2 (connected presence)" at **v0.0.3**, in active development. The landing page badges Hestia **Running**, and /running-now defines Running as "live instances actually exist and are in day-to-day use." When I looked for the substance behind that, it is "Claude Code plugin deployed on 4 machines" — which, reading between the lines, are the authors' own machines. That is disclosed, and I found it, but the front-page badge lands much harder than a v0.0.3 on four in-house machines.

### ATP / Energy (/atp-economics) (54:00)
- "It's a usage budget, not a tradeable asset. No market, no price, no speculation. You can't buy or sell ATP." I came in braced for a token pitch. This is not that, and it says so unprompted.
- The 5% transfer skim going to a community redistribution pool rather than being burned or captured, and the quality ramp (below the bar you earn nothing; ~30% earns nearly nothing, 70%+ earns a full recharge, roughly a 7× gap) make the anti-spam claim mechanical rather than aspirational.
- Confusion points: "Run out of energy budget? You die." stopped me. Then: "if you built trust, you're reborn with a head start," and elsewhere "closer to a suspended license reinstated than a clean slate. Bad actors who never earned that trust? They die for good."
- The problem is that "never earned that trust" and "bad actor" are not the same population. A brand-new honest user has not earned trust yet either — and /why-web4 told me plainly that "the first ~30 actions are the most fragile period." So: if I join, misjudge my budget in my first week, and hit zero before I have banked any trust, am I permanently dead? The site answers this for bad actors and for established users, and leaves the newcomer — the person actually reading the page — in the gap.

### What Could Go Wrong (/what-could-go-wrong) (57:00)
- "Every system has failure modes. Hiding them doesn't make them go away." Eight risks, each rated by severity *and* likelihood, and the top one is "Nobody adopts it (Existential · High)." Leading your own risk page with "this might just never catch on" is a real credibility move.
- Then seven explicitly unsolved problems: witness bootstrapping, appeals untested with real humans, governance at scale, real-world validation, regulatory ambiguity, right-to-be-forgotten with no clean answer, multi-device sync after partitions.
- This page is why my answer to "would recommend" is yes. A project that publishes its own unsolved list is one I can evaluate.

## Honest Assessment

This is the clearest explainer of a complex technical idea I have read in a long time, and I say that as someone who arrived knowing nothing. The specific things that worked:

**It respected my time.** Multiple pages told me their own length and reading order, and gave me permission to stop. /why-web4 offered me two shorter alternatives *in its own opening paragraph*, which is nearly self-sabotaging behavior for a web page and it made me trust the page more, not less.

**Plain English came first, every time.** Every acronym arrived after its meaning, demoted to a "Shorthand:" line. One of them was explicitly labeled as jargon I could ignore. I never felt tested.

**The one-moment vignette is the best teaching device on the site.** Four mechanisms, one tap of *reply*, all firing at once. Concepts became a machine there.

**It argues fairly.** Failed alternatives got a "what it gets right" before their failure. The social-credit comparison ends by admitting a Web4 society *could* be configured oppressively. A number I spot-checked across two pages matched, and the page explaining it told me why 0.7 rather than 0.5 or 0.9 — and then said outright that it is a design choice, not a physical constant.

**It publishes its own unsolved problems**, rates its own top risk as "nobody adopts it — Existential · High," and refuses to give a timeline on principle. That is the behavior of people who would rather be right than sell me something.

Now the honest part. The site's entire differentiator is that it tells the truth about maturity, so its failures on that axis cost more than they would anywhere else — and I hit one. /lct-explainer told me the hardware layer is the proven foundation while simulation is the soft part above it; /running-now told me the hardware binding is precisely the mocked part. Both pages are trying hard to be careful. One of them, in a sentence written to be scrupulous, links me straight to the other. Following the site's own careful cross-reference is how I found the contradiction, which is a peculiarly self-inflicted wound.

And downstream of that sits the bigger thing I had to work out myself: hardware-bound identity is pitched as the mechanism that makes fake identities expensive, and it is not working in any tier I could run today. Hestia defers it to hardbound; hardbound has not validated it on-device. Every one of those facts is disclosed, honestly, on the page where it belongs. But no page performs the addition. A site this committed to honest status should say the uncomfortable conjunction out loud, because a reader who assembles it independently — as I did — wonders briefly whether the distribution across three pages was the point. I do not think it was. The rest of the site's conduct argues strongly that it was not. That is exactly why closing it is worth doing: it is the only place where this site reads like every other project's site.

Smaller version of the same pattern: a topic index says 12 and lists 13. Irrelevant anywhere else. On the page whose argument *is* numerical honesty, a spot-check that fails makes me quietly re-rate every other number.

What I would do next: run `hestia init` — which is a real outcome for a site whose job is teaching, not converting. I got from zero knowledge to a concrete next command in under an hour. I would recommend it, with the caveat that I would tell a friend "the hardware-binding story is genuinely unclear across two pages, so read /running-now before you believe the identity claims." That caveat is three sentences of work to eliminate, and then I would have no caveat at all.
