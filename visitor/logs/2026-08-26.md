## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- First impression: "Trust, built into the internet." is a clean hook. The framing that AI agents already write code and move money but we can't verify any of it felt immediately relevant, not sci-fi.
- The page told me exactly what to do first (2-minute intro → Why Web4 → First Contact). I appreciated being handed an order instead of a wall of cards.
- Clicked: "Web4 in 2 minutes".
- Confusion points: the four product names (Core Standard, Hub, Hestia, Hardbound) plus six concept acronyms (LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, Karma) all appear on the landing page. I skimmed past them trusting they'd be explained later, which turned out to be the right move, but a less patient visitor might bounce here.

### Web4 in 2 Minutes — /tldr (02:00)
- The Web2 → Web3 → Web4 progression is the best single explainer on the site. "A platform *declares* your reputation" vs "trust accrues from *witnessed behavior*" — I got it in one read.
- The site was upfront that this is R&D, not a finished product. That bought a lot of goodwill.
- Confusion points: "witnessed behavior" is the load-bearing phrase of the whole pitch, and after this page I still didn't know who witnesses, or why I should believe a witness. (The site does eventually answer this, but I carried the question for four more pages.)
- It took me a beat to sort out that 4-Life is the *site about* Web4, not Web4 itself. It's stated, but I had to reread the first paragraph.

### Why Web4? — /why-web4 (06:00)
- The five root causes (actions cost nothing, reputation silos, unlimited fresh starts, AI deception, platform control) are the strongest problem framing I've seen for this kind of project. The critique that CAPTCHAs and moderation "treat symptoms, not causes" rang true.
- The 72-question FAQ is genuinely impressive — nearly every objection I formed while reading had an entry. It's also a lot. I read maybe ten entries and skimmed the cluster headings.
- The honesty is unusual: "This is research, not production. We don't attach timelines because honest research doesn't have them." I trusted the site more after this page.
- Confusion points: the ceiling-vs-actual-trust distinction (a stack of devices raises your *maximum*, not your score) took me two reads. Also the acronym density is real; the page says "you can read this page cold" and that's mostly true, but I was juggling.

### First Contact — /first-contact (16:00)
- Following Alice through five acts made the abstractions concrete. Energy goes down when you act, comes back when others confirm value, trust moves in small increments — the shape of the system clicked here.
- The "raw trust" vs "effective trust" (raw × CI²) distinction was flagged in-line, which saved me from a wrong conclusion.
- Confusion points:
  - I finished the walkthrough not knowing *how* recipients "confirm quality." It's the hinge of the whole earn-back loop and here it's just a verb.
  - Act 3's spam costs −25 ATP; I couldn't tell why that number, or what mechanically detected the spam.
  - The +12 karma bonus at rebirth is labeled "not settled," which is honest, but I couldn't tell which parts of the story were rules and which were illustration.

### How It Works — /how-it-works (26:00)
- The longest, densest page, and the one that answered most of my carried questions. The birth → life → death → rebirth frame is memorable. Concrete costs (posting 10–20 ATP, reading ~free) made the economy feel real.
- The quality ramp (below 30% pays ~zero, above 70% pays near-full) finally explained why spam starves and good work doesn't.
- The four trust invariants (bounded, conserved, attenuating, local) were a nice "this is math, not vibes" moment. 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81 for a trust chain is intuitive.
- Confusion points:
  - The three-lives example shows Life 1 ending at 145 ATP with trust 0.65 — neither death condition applies. The page does say "what else ends a life is not settled," but I stared at the table for a while trying to work out what killed her. An example whose central event is unexplained is an odd choice for a worked example.
  - "VCM / recipient attestation" appears as the answer to "who confirms quality" but is used before it's defined.
  - The law oracle / SAL governance section went by fast; "alignment without compliance is acceptable; compliance without alignment is never acceptable" sounds profound but I couldn't unpack it.

### How the Pieces Compose — /onramp (34:00)
- Short and clear: one substrate (the core standard), three scales (personal / community / enterprise) of the same shape. The claim that all three consume the same libraries "rather than three private copies that drift" is a good engineering argument even I could follow.
- Confusion points: the page makes a point of the hub having "no mention of hardbound anywhere in the hub's source" as a deliberate design fact. I could not work out why that's presented as a virtue — is the hub not supposed to know about enterprises? One sentence of "why" would help.

### Running Now — /running-now (38:00)
- The Spec / Reference / Running badge system is exactly what I wanted after all the concept pages. Only Hestia is "Running," and the caveat is candid: the lab that builds it runs it on its own 4 machines. No public network exists.
- The accountability gap disclosure ("actions bypassing the policy gate leave no trace... an accepted limit right now rather than a scheduled repair") is the kind of sentence that made me trust the rest of the site.
- Confusion points: "Running" therefore means "dogfooded by its own authors," which is honest one sentence later but the badge alone reads bigger than that.

### ATP/ADP — /atp-economics (42:00)
- The recharge-vs-payment split answered my biggest open question: self-initiated work can at most refund what you spent; only *commissioned* work nets positive. That's a real answer and I wish an earlier page had said it this plainly.
- The worked example (two people spend 50 ATP; 35% quality recovers ~6, 85% recovers ~42) is exactly the right kind of concrete.
- Confusion points:
  - ADP. The page calls it "background accounting" and I finished still unable to say what an ADP *is* or why the cycle needs a second token name. If I never manage it, why do I need to know it exists — and if I need to know it exists, one diagram of the ATP→ADP→confirmation→recharge loop would do it.
  - The cold-start problem (you need confirmations to earn, and ATP to get confirmations) is flagged as open — honest, but it means the page's economy currently has no explained entry point for a newcomer with no reputation.

### LCT — /lct-explainer (47:00)
- "A digital ID card that lives on your own device" that malware can't read out — good. The private-key-never-leaves-the-chip explanation was pitched at exactly my level.
- Pseudonymity ("they see an entity with a trust history," not Jane Smith) answered a privacy worry I'd been carrying since /why-web4.
- Confusion points:
  - The page says software-only identities cap at 0.50 — and elsewhere the site told me 0.50 is the neutral starting point and the survival line, with restrictions below it and trust death if you stay low. Putting those together: a software-only participant's *ceiling* is the line everyone else merely *starts* at. Can such a participant ever dip and recover? Are they permanently one bad week from the drop-off? No page I found puts these two facts side by side and says what life at the cap actually looks like.
  - "Witness" means two things (my own devices co-signing vs network witness nodes) and the page separates them only partway through.

### Trust Tensor — /trust-tensor (52:00)
- Talent/Training/Temperament with different decay rates is genuinely elegant — talent never fades, temperament needs fresh demonstration (30-day half-life). That maps to how I actually judge people.
- Role-specificity (Alice: 90% analyst, 28% mechanic) made instant sense.
- Confusion points: the page states canonical composite weights (0.4/0.3/0.3), and its interactive widget computes a "role-weighted match" percentage using different, role-specific weights. Two weighting schemes, one page, and I couldn't tell which number the *system* uses when it decides something about me (like rebirth eligibility, which /how-it-works said uses the canonical composite). A one-liner in the widget saying "this match % is a hiring-style view; the canonical composite is the one the survival rules use" would have saved me the puzzling.

### What Could Go Wrong — /what-could-go-wrong (55:00)
- I clicked this expecting marketing dressed as candor and got an actual failure-mode analysis: nine named risks with severity/likelihood, measured bypasses in their own running prototype (with a GitHub issue number), and admissions like "no validated economic model" and simulations of only 4–200 agents.
- This page did more for my trust in the project than any explainer. Publishing "the agent routes around its own safety gate" about your own flagship running component is not something projects usually do.
- Confusion points: Risk #4's circular-farming math assumes "3 witnesses per interaction (60% base detection)" — as a skeptic I wanted to know what happens inside the undetected 40%, and the page moves on.

## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level) — trust earned from witnessed behavior instead of declared by platforms or assumed from keys
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together — one substrate, three scales
- [x] What LCT means — hardware-anchored, pseudonymous identity whose key can't leave the chip
- [x] What ATP/ADP means — mostly; ATP yes (spend to act, earn back via confirmed value, commissioned work is the only net-positive channel); ADP remains a name for "background accounting" I can't picture
- [x] What Trust Tensors are — Talent/Training/Temperament, per role, with different decay rates
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D — clearest part of the whole site; only Hestia runs, and only in its authors' lab
- [x] Why any of this matters — the five root causes on /why-web4 did this

## Unanswered Questions
1. What is everyday participation like for a software-only identity whose *ceiling* is the 0.50 line everyone else merely starts at? Can they dip and come back?
2. What actually ends a life when neither death condition triggers (the three-lives example)? The site says "not settled" — settled or not, what did the *example* assume?
3. What does the undetected fraction of witness-collusion (the 40% in Risk #4) buy a patient attacker over time?
4. Which number governs me on /trust-tensor — the canonical composite or the widget's role-match — in which situations?
5. What is an ADP, concretely? One sentence that isn't "background accounting."
6. How does the very first newcomer with zero reputation get commissioned work (the site flags this cold-start as open — fair — but it means I can't yet picture my own first week).

## Honest Assessment
This is the most honest project website I can remember reading. Twice I went looking for the catch — the "Running" badge and the What Could Go Wrong page — and both times the site had already confessed more than I would have found on my own: the lab dogfoods its own software on four machines, the flagship prototype's safety gate can be routed around, the simulations top out at a couple hundred agents, the economics are unvalidated. That candor is the site's best feature and the reason I'd come back.

The teaching mostly works. The guided path (2 minutes → why → Alice) is well ordered: problem first, then story, then mechanics. By /how-it-works I could predict what a page was about to tell me, which is the sign the earlier pages did their job.

Where it still creaks is at the seams between pages. Each page is internally careful, but the facts a reader carries *across* pages sometimes collide with no one there to referee: the 0.50 software cap vs the 0.50 survival line; the widget's weights vs the canonical weights; "recipients confirm value" as a verb on one page and a mechanism three pages later; a worked example whose lives end for a reason the site says is unsettled. None of these broke my understanding, but each one cost me minutes of private reconciliation that a single cross-referencing sentence would have saved.

The other honest note: this site taught me a system that, by its own account, I cannot join. That's disclosed everywhere and I respect it, but it shapes the visit — I left feeling like I'd read an excellent textbook about a country that doesn't have a border crossing yet. I'd return when there's a door.
