## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- **First impression**: "Trust, built into the internet." plus "A proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority, and by what rules." That is a good headline. The word "proposed" in the second line did a lot of work for me: I knew within five seconds that I was not being sold a product. The AI-agent framing landed, because I have genuinely wondered who is accountable when an agent does something on my behalf.
- The four cards (core standard / hub / hestia / hardbound) with one line each were the right amount. I did not understand them yet but I understood *that there are four things*.
- **Clicked**: "Web4 in 2 Minutes", because it was the smallest promise on the page.
- **Confusion points**: the site is called "4-Life" and nothing on the page says why. I assumed it meant "for life" (as in permanent reputation) and I was wrong-ish; the site turns out to use "life", "lives", "aliveness" and "rebirth" as technical terms, so the name is probably a pun on that. I never found out. See friction log.

### Web4 in 2 Minutes (03:00)
- This is not a 2-minute page. `/learn` bills it at "2 min". I would put it at 5 to 6 minutes of actual reading. Not a disaster, but the one number on the site whose job is to set expectations about time is the one that was off.
- Content-wise this was strong. The Web2 / Web3 / Web4 three-way contrast is the single clearest thing on the site: "a platform declares your reputation" vs "whoever holds the key is assumed to be the rightful owner" vs "trust accrues from witnessed behavior". I understood the pitch from that alone.
- The badge key (Spec / Reference / Running) appearing *this early* was smart. And the line "The daily use counted so far is the building lab's own, so this badge does not claim adoption" made me trust the site more than anything else I read all session. Somebody wrote that sentence against their own interest.
- **Clicked**: "Read the site in order (recommended)" to `/learn`.

### The Reading Path (09:00)

- The progress tracker ("0 / 24 concepts", kept in this browser only) is a nice touch and the privacy note is unprompted and welcome.

- **Clicked**: step 2, Why Web4?

### Why Web4? (13:00)
- The problem framing is excellent and I did not need any prior knowledge: actions have no cost, reputation is platform-trapped, identity reset is trivial, AI deception is accelerating, platforms hold absolute control. All five landed.
- Then I hit the FAQ and fell in. **It is about 55 questions across 7 categories.** `/learn` calls this "6 min + optional Q&A", which is technically fenced, but the Q&A is several times the length of the page it hangs off. I read maybe fifteen of them and started skimming.
- While skimming I hit the thing that actually broke my trust for a minute: **the same question is answered twice, with different attitudes.** There is a "What about people who can't afford devices with security chips?" and a separate "What about people who can't afford devices with TPM chips?". One answer opens with, in effect, this is not really a barrier ("Hardware-bound identity does not require a security chip... What hardware buys is ceiling, not entry"). The other opens with "This is a genuine equity concern". Both are on the page. As a reader I could not tell which one is the site's position, and I could not tell which one was written later. Same shape for "Can't someone with lots of hardware create many identities?" versus "Can someone buy 50 devices to create a super-trusted identity?", and for a couple of the opt-in/Web2-transition pairs.
- One question asks "Why these specific numbers? How were 0.5, 3 hops, and 0.02 chosen?" I had met 0.5 and 3 hops by then. I had never seen 0.02 anywhere. The question assumes I know a number the page has not shown me.
- Genuinely good moment: the page volunteers that software-only identities top out at 0.50 and that 0.50 is *also* the survival line. It says out loud that this is "a second-class experience baked into the architecture" and links to its own risk register. I did not expect a "why you should want this" page to hand me that.

### First Contact (26:00)
- I liked this a lot. "Only two numbers move while she acts" is exactly the right way to open an interactive thing. Energy Budget starts at 100, Trust starts at 0.50, and that is all I have to hold.
- The five-act arc (Join / Build / Stumble / Recover / Die and Reborn) is a good story shape. The "Suspended, not deleted" callout pre-empted my exact worry.
- The "About that number" callout on the +12 karma bonus, saying "12 is one modelling choice, not the rule. Read it for the shape... not the amount", is honest and I appreciated it.
- **But**: the whole walkthrough quietly assumes something it never tells you. Alice climbs from 0.50 to 0.56 to 0.62. Two pages later I learned that a software-only identity has a hard ceiling of 0.50, so *that climb is impossible for a software-only user*. And `/karma-consequences` says karma rebirth only describes hardware-anchored tiers ("at that tier the record stays sheddable and this walkthrough does not describe it"). So both of the things First Contact dramatizes are hardware-tier-only. The words "hardware", "chip", "TPM", "ceiling", and "software-only" do not appear anywhere on the First Contact page. I checked. Alice's tier is never stated. I walked away from page 3 of 5 believing a story that does not apply to the tier I would actually be on if I installed anything today.

### How It Works (38:00)
- The most content-dense page on the site, and the one where I got confused in a way I could not resolve without leaving the reading path.
- **The Key Takeaways box told me the wrong words.** It says "Your trust is multi-dimensional (competence, reliability, consistency) and role-specific." Everywhere else on the site (including further down this same page) it is Talent, Training, Temperament. The Key Takeaways box is the first thing I read on the page. I learned "competence, reliability, consistency", carried it for several minutes, and then had to unlearn it. That is the one thing on the whole site that actively taught me something false.
- **The rebirth rule contradicts First Contact.** First Contact told me: everyone starts a life with 100, karma is added on top, Alice restarts at 112. This page tells me, twice, with arithmetic behind it: "Life 1 → Life 2: Ended with 145 ATP. Reborn with 145 ATP (karma carried forward)" and "Life 2 → Life 3: Ended with 130 ATP. Reborn with 130 ATP". Those are not the same rule. One is "reset to 100, add a bonus", the other is "keep everything you had". This page states its version flat, with no hedge at all.
- This mattered to me beyond pedantry: under the version on this page, **dying costs you nothing**. You keep your entire balance. The site's whole thesis is that consequences are real and behavior follows you, and page 4 of the recommended path accidentally says death is free.
- I only found out this was a *known* open question by clicking into `/karma-consequences`, which is not on the five-page path. That page says plainly: "Whether you keep your whole final balance or a reduced portion of it is not decided, and the lifecycle walkthrough's karma examples model it the other way." So the site knows. It just tells you on a page the reading path does not send you to. First Contact's hedge only covers the *amount* of karma (the 12), not the fact that there are two competing models.
- Otherwise this page is good: the SAL framework, the plagiarism walkthrough with the appeal path, the four trust invariants (boundedness / conservation / transitive attenuation / locality), and the 5-hop 0.9-trust chain decaying to 0.59 all made sense and were concrete.

### Running Now (52:00)
- **Best page on the site.** No notes, only praise. The badge key is defined before it is used. "Pilot-ready" is explicitly called out as *not* a badge but a statement made inside Reference. Version numbers are given. Test counts are given, and one of them is actively disclaimed ("The 2,627-test figure you will see quoted belongs to a third artifact"). A page whose job is to say what works spends a paragraph telling you where its own audit trail has a hole.
- "Where you fit today" (find the row that sounds like you) is the best navigation element I hit all session, including a row for "You are here to understand it, not to run it" which is exactly me.

## Honest Assessment

This is the best-written explainer site I have read for something this abstract, and the reason is not the explanations. It is the disclosure. Almost every page volunteers a limitation before I could think to ask for it: the Running badge counts only the lab's own machines, the 2,627-test figure belongs to a different artifact, the audit chain records governed activity rather than all activity, two environment variables get you past the policy gate, the appeals process has never met a real human. `/running-now` and `/what-could-go-wrong` are genuinely unusual documents. By the end I trusted the site more than I trusted the technology, which I think is the correct outcome for something honestly labelled R&D.

The failures I hit are almost all of one kind, and it is worth naming the pattern rather than the instances: **the honest version of a claim exists on the site, but not on the page where a first-time reader meets the claim.** The two rebirth models are disclosed on `/karma-consequences`, not on `/how-it-works` where the contradicting numbers live. The hardware ceiling that makes Alice's story tier-specific is on `/lct-explainer` and `/what-could-go-wrong`, not on `/first-contact` where the story is told. The equity framing of the hardware question exists in the FAQ, next to a second copy of the same question that frames it as a non-issue. The site's care is real; it is just distributed unevenly, and the reading path does not always pass through the careful version.

The Key Takeaways slip on `/how-it-works` is different and I would fix it first because it is a five-word change. Everything else on that page taught me correctly. That one box taught me "competence, reliability, consistency" and I believed it, because it was the first thing on the page and it was in a box labelled Key Takeaways.

The thing I keep coming back to: page 3 sold me on a story, and page 4 quietly told me the story's ending was free. If death costs nothing, I do not know why I should care about any of the mechanisms. I do not think that is what the site believes. I think a worked example got written once and never reconciled. But I read it in order, the way the site told me to, and that is the order it happened in.

I would come back. I would tell someone interested in AI accountability to read `/running-now` and `/what-could-go-wrong` and skip the rest until they are hooked.
