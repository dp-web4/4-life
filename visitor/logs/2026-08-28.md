## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level): a proposed standard where trust comes from witnessed actions rather than a platform's say-so or a key. The Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast on /tldr taught me this.
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together: one substrate, three scales. The site taught me this well; /onramp's "there is no hub-hardbound seam" made it feel real.
- [x] What LCT means: a device-anchored identity, witnessed, pseudonymous, with a trust ceiling set by hardware. Taught. What I did NOT get: whether the ceiling is set by chip type or device count.
- [ ] What ATP/ADP means: partially. A per-person energy budget you spend to act; ADP is the receipt. Taught. But the site's headline ("quality earns energy back") and its mechanics ("your own work refunds at most its cost") disagree, and I only understood the real rule on page 14.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are: three numbers (Talent, Training, Temperament), weighted per role, 0.4/0.3/0.3. Taught, with arithmetic I could check.
- [x] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D: this is the site's best subject. /running-now is the page I'd cite.
- [x] Why any of this matters: yes. "You're a guest in someone else's house" and "you can't escape your credit history by changing your name" did it.

Things I inferred rather than was taught: that "witnessing is per action" means no permanent witnesses; that the reason so many numbers are "4-Life's calibration" is that the standard deliberately leaves them to each society (I pieced this together from /atp-economics's "issuance is society law" and /trust-tensor's "the standard leaves those to each society"). Nobody said that in one place.

## Honest Assessment
This is the most honest project website I have ever read, and that honesty is both its strength and its main usability problem.

The strength: I never once felt sold to. Every page says what is real. /running-now names version numbers and test counts and says "4 machines, all the lab's own." /what-could-go-wrong's Risk 9 admits the one running piece can be bypassed with two environment variables and links the bug. The tldr says "No timeline commitments. This is research, and research takes as long as it takes." I came in expecting a crypto pitch and got a lab notebook. I'd trust these people.

The problem: by page four I had counted nine things the site told me were "not settled", "a modelling choice", "ours, not the protocol's", or "a teaching calibration": the karma amount, trust at rebirth, what else ends a life, how long "stays below" is, the decay half-lives, the per-hop decay, the transfer fee, the starting balance, and the first commission. Each admission is correct and I'd rather have it than not. But the beginner path presents three worked examples (Alice, the three-lives agent, the karma agent) that use three different toy rules, and each page apologizes for the others. The cumulative effect is that I finished knowing what Web4 IS (I really do) and not knowing what any of its numbers ARE, which makes the many worked examples feel like fiction with footnotes. One toy arithmetic, used everywhere, with one sentence saying "the real value is up to each society," would have taught me the same shape with a third of the doubt.

The single biggest thing I learned late was the recharge-vs-payment rule. "Quality earns energy back" is the site's most repeated sentence. The mechanics say your own work at best breaks even and growth only comes from being commissioned, and getting a first commission is unsolved. That is a much more sober story than "spam dies, quality compounds," and it's the true one according to the site's own pages. It belongs on page 3, not page 14.

The pages I'd send someone: /tldr, /running-now, /what-could-go-wrong. The page I'd fix first: /first-contact, because it's where the numbers start and where the reconciliation should happen. The thing that most made me want to leave: the glossary's Advanced section. The thing that most made me want to stay: Risk 9.

Would I return? Maybe, when there's a network to join. Would I recommend it? To a developer who runs agents, yes, today: hestia's "A Day" section is concrete. To my non-technical sister, no, not yet; she'd stop at the eleven acronyms.
