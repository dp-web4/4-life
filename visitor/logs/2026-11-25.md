## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- First impression: "Trust, built into the internet." Clear headline. The pitch underneath is concrete: AI agents take real actions and nobody can prove what they did, on whose authority, or under what rules. I understood the problem in ten seconds.
- "Witness" is defined right there (observe an action and attest to it, with your own reputation at stake). Good.
- The four cards (Core Standard, Hub, Hestia, Hardbound) with scale labels (substrate / community / personal / enterprise) gave me a shape, though the card text is already dense: "seven roles, a signed founding law, an append-only witnessed ledger, sealed member channels" and "a cryptographic LCT". LCT is not expanded on the card.
- "The vocabulary" row is six buttons of acronyms: LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, Karma. Each has a one-word gloss (Identity, Trust, Value, Neighborhood, Energy, Reputation), which helps, but I had no idea why trust and value are "T3" and "V3".
- "Where this actually stands" says plainly this is R&D. I liked that.
- Clicked: "Web4 in 2 minutes".

### /tldr (03:00)
- This was the best page on the site for me. Web2 = platform declares your reputation. Web3 = whoever holds the key is assumed to be the owner. Web4 = trust accrues from witnessed behavior. I got it.
- The badge key (Spec / Reference / Running) is clear and unusually honest: "The daily use counted so far is the building lab's own, so this badge does not claim adoption."
- Confusion: the page promises "Every term is spelled out the first time it appears". True for the words it uses. But it never uses LCT, T3, ATP, "trust tensor" or "witness chain" at all. That mattered later (see /hub, /hestia, /hardbound, /the-standard).
- "Hestia runs end to end today" and the Running badge made me think there is something people use. I only learned on /running-now that this means 4 machines belonging to the lab.
- Clicked: "Why Web4?" from Related Concepts.

- The FAQ: the page says 72 questions. I did not read all of them. I skimmed the "Most asked" 12 and maybe 25 of the rest. They are frank ("Honest caveat" on nearly every one), which builds trust, but several contradict each other or other pages:
  - "As few as 5-10 active members" (most asked) vs "3-5 people is enough" (going deeper).

So a sabbatical puts a top performer exactly on the death line, and anyone less than stellar under it. The site knows this is open, but it is a big thing to leave open.

- [x] Why any of this matters: yes for AI-agent accountability (the landing pitch) and for spam/sybil problems (/why-web4). Partly inferred: the site has two pitches (prove what AI agents did; fix social-media reputation) and never says which one it is mainly about.

## Honest Assessment
The site is at its best when it is short and when it is admitting something. /tldr gave me the whole idea in two minutes. /running-now told me exactly how little is deployed, down to "two environment variables bypass the gate". The "Honest caveat" lines, the "what's settled vs still moving" box, and the appeals section made me trust the authors more than I trust most project sites. I have rarely seen a project say "this policy choice is contestable and falls hardest on people with the least hardware" about its own design.

It is at its worst when it shows numbers. I am not a specialist, but I can do arithmetic, and almost every worked example I checked failed: the quality ramp table against its own rule, the Novice's trust climb against the update formula, the karma sign across pages, the detection percentage in the LCT widget. The one that closed cleanly (Alice's three roles on /trust-tensor) felt like relief. The site's habit when numbers disagree is to add a note saying they disagree and that the real value is unsettled. After the fourth such note I stopped reading numbers as information and started reading them as decoration. If the amounts are not settled, I would rather see fewer of them, consistently, than many of them with apologies.

There is also a gap between the two halves of the site. The "onramp" half (standard, hub, Hestia, Hardbound) is about real software for governing AI agents on real machines, and it is written for developers who already know the vocabulary. The "concepts" half (ATP, karma, death and rebirth, trust neighborhoods) describes an internet-scale social system that the site says exists only in simulation. The pages link to each other constantly, but I could not find the sentence that says how the second grows out of the first, or whether Hestia today uses ATP, karma or death at all. I suspect it does not. If so, a newcomer reading First Contact is learning a world that the runnable software does not yet implement, and that should be said at the top.

Emotionally: the energy-death framing made me anxious rather than intrigued. Being charged to read, never profiting from my own posts, losing standing if I take a break, and being "reborn" with a handicap: taken together it sounds like a place I would be nervous to participate in.
