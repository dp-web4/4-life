## Journey

### Landing Page (00:00)
- First impression: clean and direct. "Trust, built into the internet." and the subheadline "A proposed open standard for proving what an AI agent did, on whose authority, and by what rules" told me what this is about in one breath. The three-sentence problem statement (agents write code, move money; we cannot prove what they did; Web4 closes that gap) is the best thing on the site.
- "To witness is to observe an action and attest to it, staking your own reputation on the record." Good, a definition right when I needed it.
- Then the four cards hit: "open ontology", "witnessed presence, role-contextual trust", "seven roles, a signed founding law, an append-only witnessed ledger, and sealed member channels", "cryptographic LCT". I understood maybe half of the nouns. I got the shape (one standard, three sizes: personal / community / enterprise) but not the content.
- "Where this actually stands" is refreshingly blunt: "Web4 is research and development, not a finished product."
- Confusion points:
  - There is a link "what does that tag mean?" but I did not see any tag on the landing page to wonder about. A question about something I had not been shown.
  - "The vocabulary" section lists LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, Karma as bare labels. Six acronyms at once with no expansion.
  - "The hub is pilot-ready" vs "Hestia runs end to end today" vs "Hardbound is a usable, actively-hardened enterprise tier". Three different status phrasings and I cannot rank them.
- Clicked: "Web4 in 2 minutes".

Both are sort of explained if I go read the fine print, but as a newcomer I read these two sentences five minutes apart and thought one of them was wrong.

## Understanding Gained
By the end of this session, I understood:
- [x] What Web4 is (at a high level) — taught by / and /tldr: a proposed standard so that trust is earned from witnessed behavior instead of declared by a platform or assumed from a key.
- [x] What the onramp is (the core standard, the hub, hestia, hardbound) and how they fit together — taught at the level of "one base, three sizes". How they connect technically (/onramp) went over my head.
- [x] What LCT means — Linked Context Token; an identity anchored to a chip in my device. Taught. The ID format itself I could not reconcile.
- [~] What ATP/ADP means — the concept yes (energy budget; spend makes a receipt; confirmations refund; commissions pay). The numbers no: the quality ramp does not add up and spam is the cheapest action.
- [x] What Trust Tensors are — three scores (Talent, Training, Temperament) per role. Taught well. What number the 0.50 line reads, and what a capped software user can do, I did not get.
- [~] What is real and runnable today vs still R&D — /running-now taught it, but I had to unlearn the plain meaning of "Running" and "deployed" first, and I still cannot tell whether the lab's "live society" is a hub.
- [x] Why any of this matters — the agent-accountability framing on the landing page and the problem list on /why-web4 both landed.

## Honest Assessment
The first ten minutes are good. The landing page headline, the three-sentence problem, and the Web2/Web3/Web4 contrast on /tldr gave me a mental model I could say out loud, and the site told me the truth about its own status more often than any project page I can remember. The repeated "this is R&D", "the lab's own use", "not validated on real devices", "stops accidents, not adversaries" made me trust the authors.

The trouble is that the honesty is carried by caveats layered on top of confident sentences, and the two layers often arrive in the wrong order or on different pages. "Running" then "no real users". "We run one" then "does not claim any exist". Present-tense federation then "Spec only". "Proving what an agent did" then "writes no entry at all". I kept having to revise what I had just learned. By the end I was reading every strong claim expecting the retraction, which is tiring and probably not the feeling the site wants.

The second trouble is numbers. The site loves worked examples, and most of the arithmetic is correct when I check it. But the examples disagree with each other across pages in ways that matter to the story: death by exhaustion is a penalty, a bonus, or neutral; the quality ramp table matches neither of its own descriptions; spam is the cheapest thing on the menu; the count of core systems changes mid-page. Several pages openly say "another page models this differently". I would rather be given one consistent toy model and one sentence saying "these numbers are illustrative" than three models each apologizing for the others. The "settled vs still moving" box on /atp-economics is exactly the right instrument; it should be the pattern everywhere.

The third thing is the 0.50 line. It is the centre of the whole trust story (start at 0.50, below it you are restricted, stay below and you die), and it is also exactly where the site caps anyone without special hardware, which today is everyone using the one piece that runs. The site knows this is unresolved and says so. But as a newcomer the takeaway I walked off with is "if I try this, I start pinned to the edge of the cliff", and I doubt that is the intended lesson.

Who is it for? Pages 1-3 are for a curious general reader. From /onramp onward it is for a developer comfortable with Rust, TPMs and RDF. I am the first kind; I would come back if someone told me the numbers had been reconciled, or if there were something to look at that is not a terminal.
